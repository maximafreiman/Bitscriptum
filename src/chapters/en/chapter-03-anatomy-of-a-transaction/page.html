Chapter 3 · 3.1
Not a balance transfer, but a cryptographic chain
What is a Transaction?

If you’ve ever transferred money through a bank, you’re used to a simple model: a balance decreases in one account, increases in another. The bank records the change. Done.

Bitcoin doesn’t work that way.

In Bitcoin, there’s no such thing as a "balance." There is no number stored somewhere that reads "you have 1 BTC." What exists is a history of transactions, and from that history is calculated how much Bitcoin you can use right now.

A transaction is a transfer instruction

Every Bitcoin transaction is a cryptographically signed message containing two main things: where this Bitcoin comes from, and where this Bitcoin is going.

"Where it comes from" is a reference to a previous transaction that gave Bitcoin to you. "Where it goes" is an instruction for whom this Bitcoin is sent to and how much.

The network verifies that you really have the right to move the Bitcoin you reference, then broadcasts the transaction to all nodes. If it’s valid, it enters a block and becomes a permanent part of the blockchain.

Not a number, but a chain

This is what makes Bitcoin fundamentally different from conventional financial systems. Your ownership isn’t a number in a database. It’s a cryptographic chain that can be traced all the way back to the first block, the Genesis Block, mined by Satoshi in January 2009.

Every Bitcoin in this world can have its origin traced. Nothing can appear out of nothing.

In Bitcoin, "owning" Bitcoin means having the ability to sign a transaction that references an unspent output. Not a number in a database, but a key that unlocks an output. This concept will become much clearer in 3.2.

Chapter 3 · 3.2
Digital banknotes that can’t be cut
UTXO — Unspent Transaction Output

UTXO stands for Unspent Transaction Output. This is the most important concept in Chapter 3, and perhaps one of the most frequently misunderstood concepts in Bitcoin.

Forget the concept of a balance

In the banking system, your ownership is represented as a number: a balance. The bank stores that number in their database and changes it every time there’s a transaction.

Bitcoin has no balance. What Bitcoin has is a collection of unspent transaction outputs. Each output has a certain value and can only be used once, by the person who has the right key. That is a UTXO.

The banknote analogy

The easiest way to understand a UTXO is to imagine physical banknotes. If you have a Rp 100.000 note, you don’t have "a balance of Rp 100.000." You have a single piece of paper worth Rp 100.000 that physically sits in your hand.

If you want to pay Rp 30.000, you can’t cut the banknote. You hand over the entire Rp 100.000, the merchant takes Rp 30.000, and returns Rp 70.000 to you. A UTXO works exactly like that. You can’t spend a portion. You have to spend the whole thing, and the remainder comes back to you as a new UTXO.

What a Bitcoin "balance" really is

If your wallet shows "0.55 BTC," that’s not one number in one place. It might be the sum of several UTXOs: for example one UTXO worth 0.05 BTC from Alice, one worth 0.12 BTC from Bob, one worth 0.08 BTC from a mining reward, and one worth 0.30 BTC from Charlie. Your wallet adds up all the UTXOs you can sign for, and displays them as a single number.

Why UTXO?

The UTXO model has several advantages. First, it makes verification easy: to check whether a transaction is valid, a node only needs to make sure the referenced UTXO exists and hasn’t been used. Second, it prevents double-spending naturally: once a UTXO is used, it disappears from the active UTXO set and can’t be used again.

The entire state of Bitcoin can be summarized in one set of data: the UTXO set. This is the list of all unspent transaction outputs across the whole network. As of mid-2026 its size is around 11 GB, holding more than 170 million UTXOs. Every full node stores it and uses it to validate every new transaction.

Who picks the UTXOs for you?

In the simulation above you chose which UTXOs to spend yourself. In everyday use you never do that. You type an amount and an address, then press send. But the decision still has to be made by someone, and that someone is your wallet. The process is called coin selection, and it explains a lot of the things that feel strange to new users.

Why does it matter? Because as we saw in 3.4, the cost of a transaction is set by its shape, not its value. Every UTXO drawn in adds one input, and every input adds size. Picking three small UTXOs instead of one large one can multiply the fee for a payment of exactly the same amount.

A wallet is juggling goals that pull against each other. It wants the fee as low as possible, which argues for using as few inputs as it can. It also wants to avoid leaving change that is too small, because tiny change today becomes an expensive UTXO to spend tomorrow. And ideally it leaks no more information than necessary, because every pair of UTXOs combined in one transaction tells the world they belong to the same person. We look at the privacy consequences in Chapter 10.

The ideal outcome is finding a combination that adds up exactly, so no change output is needed at all. The transaction comes out smaller, cheaper, and leaves no change trail to analyse. Bitcoin Core calls this search branch and bound, and it does try for that exact match first. The trouble is such matches are rare, so when the search fails the wallet falls back to methods that accept change.

This is where several things that puzzle new users start to make sense. Why is sending a small amount sometimes far more expensive than usual? Because your funds are scattered across many tiny UTXOs and the wallet is forced to combine a lot of inputs. Why can the fee differ when the amount sent is identical? Because the shape of the transaction differs, even though the value does not.

It is also why many wallets offer a way to pick UTXOs by hand, usually called coin control. For ordinary use, letting the wallet decide is fine. But once you care about privacy, or you are facing high fees, taking that decision back can matter a great deal. And if your funds are already scattered across many small UTXOs, there is a specific way to tidy them up that we cover in 10.4.

Chapter 3 · 3.3
The anatomy inside a transaction
Inputs and Outputs

Every Bitcoin transaction has two main parts: inputs and outputs. An input is a reference to a UTXO you consume. An output is an instruction for where Bitcoin goes after this transaction.

Input

An input isn’t a number. An input is a pointer to an unspent output of a previous transaction. It contains two things: a reference to which UTXO is consumed, and proof that you have the right to use it in the form of a cryptographic signature from your private key.

A single transaction can have many inputs. This happens when you need to combine several small UTXOs to pay a larger amount, like combining several small banknotes to pay for something more expensive.

Output

An output determines where Bitcoin goes. Each output contains two things: the amount of BTC sent, and the condition that must be met to spend this output in the future. That condition is usually simple: anyone who can prove they hold the private key matching this address may spend this output.

A single transaction can also have many outputs. You can send to several addresses at once in one transaction, and the change output back to yourself also counts as a separate output.

The hidden fee

There’s one interesting thing: the miner fee isn’t written explicitly inside the transaction. The fee is the difference between the total input value and the total output value. If you have inputs worth 0.1 BTC and create outputs worth 0.099 BTC, then the remaining 0.001 BTC automatically becomes the fee for the miner who includes your transaction in a block.

This is why it’s so important to always check the total output before signing a transaction. If you forget to create a change output and only create one output to the recipient, the entire difference becomes the miner fee. There are real cases where someone paid thousands of dollars in fees because they forgot to add a change output.

Beyond amounts and destinations, a transaction can also carry conditions about time: the earliest it may enter a block, or how long a UTXO must wait before it can be spent. Bitcoin has four mechanisms for this, and we take all of them apart in Chapter 15.

Chapter 3 · 3.4
Not a tariff, but an offer to miners
Transaction Fee

Fees in Bitcoin don’t work like a bank’s transfer fee. There’s no fixed tariff, no percentage of the amount sent, and nothing sets it centrally.

Fees in Bitcoin are determined by the market.

How fees are calculated

In Bitcoin, fees are calculated based on the size of the transaction in bytes, not based on the value of the Bitcoin sent. Sending 1 BTC and sending 0.00001 BTC can have exactly the same fee if the transaction size is the same.

The unit used is satoshis per vbyte (sat/vB). One satoshi is the smallest unit of Bitcoin: 0.00000001 BTC. One vbyte is the transaction size in virtual bytes, a unit that accounts for the efficiency of modern transaction formats.

Why fees rise and fall

Each Bitcoin block has limited capacity, about 1-4 MB per block, and a new block appears on average every 10 minutes. When many people want to transact at once, the space in a block becomes contested.

Miners choose which transactions go into their block. They’ll prioritize transactions offering the highest fee per vbyte because that maximizes their revenue. Transactions with low fees will wait longer, or even not make it into a block at all if the network is very busy.

The relationship between fees and UTXOs

The more inputs in a transaction, the larger the transaction size in bytes, the higher the fee that needs to be paid. This is why modern wallets try to manage UTXOs efficiently, consolidating small UTXOs when fees are cheap, so they don’t need to use many inputs when fees are expensive.

RBF (Replace by Fee)

If your transaction has already been broadcast to the network but hasn’t entered a block and its fee is too low, there’s a mechanism called RBF, or Replace by Fee. You can resend the same transaction with a higher fee, and miners will prioritize the new version. One condition is easy to miss: the original transaction must have been marked as replaceable when it was created. If it wasn’t marked, many nodes will reject the replacement. The details of the mechanism, including what to do if you are the recipient rather than the sender, we cover in 9.3.

Fees aren’t a charge taken by "Bitcoin." A fee is an incentive you offer to miners so they prioritize your transaction. No company, no platform, no third party receives it. The fee goes directly to the miner who finds your block.

Chapter 3 · 3.5
The queue before entering the blockchain
Mempool

When you send a Bitcoin transaction, it doesn’t go straight into the blockchain. It goes into a queue first.

That queue is called the mempool, short for memory pool. Every node in the Bitcoin network keeps its own mempool, a list of transactions that have been broadcast, validated, but not yet confirmed into a block.

The journey of a transaction

Once you sign and broadcast a transaction, it spreads to all nodes in the network within seconds through a process called gossiping. Each node forwards valid transactions to its neighboring nodes. But entering the mempool doesn’t mean it’s done. Your transaction still waits there until a miner decides to include it in the block they’re mining.

Miners choose transactions

Miners aren’t required to include every transaction in the mempool. They choose which transactions yield the highest total fee for their block. The most common strategy is to pick the transactions with the highest fee per vbyte first until the block’s capacity is full. This is why the fee rate you choose determines how quickly your transaction is confirmed.

The mempool can fill up

When the network is very busy, the mempool can swell to hundreds of megabytes. Transactions with fees that are too low can get stuck there for hours, even days. Some nodes will even drop transactions with very low fees from their mempool when memory is full.

Confirmation

Once your transaction enters a block, it gets one confirmation. Each new block mined on top of that block adds one more confirmation. The more confirmations, the harder it is to reverse that transaction, the more final your transaction. For small everyday transactions, one confirmation is usually enough. For large transactions, most people wait for 6 confirmations before considering a transaction truly final.

The mempool isn’t one global queue that’s the same for everyone. Each node has its own mempool with rules and capacity that can differ. But because nodes share transactions with one another, in practice most nodes have similar mempool contents.

Chapter 3 · 3.6
From creation to final
The Lifecycle of a Transaction

We’ve learned all the components: UTXO, inputs, outputs, fees, the mempool, confirmation. Now let’s put it all together in one complete picture.

This is the full journey of a Bitcoin transaction, from beginning to end.

1. Creation

The wallet chooses UTXOs sufficient to cover the amount to be sent plus the fee. It assembles a transaction with inputs referencing those UTXOs, an output to the recipient, and a change output back to the sender if there’s a remainder.

2. Signing

The sender’s private key is used to create a cryptographic signature that proves ownership of the referenced UTXO. This signature is unique to this transaction and can’t be reused for another transaction.

3. Broadcast

The signed transaction is broadcast to the network. The first node that receives it validates: do its UTXOs exist and are they unspent? Is the signature valid? If yes, it forwards it to its neighboring nodes.

4. Mempool

The transaction enters the mempool on every node that receives it. It waits there alongside thousands of other transactions. The fee rate determines how quickly a miner picks it.

5. Miner selection

When a miner is assembling a new block, it chooses transactions from the mempool based on the highest fee per vbyte. Your transaction enters the candidate block being mined.

6. Block found

The miner finds a valid proof of work and broadcasts the block to the network. Other nodes verify it and add it to the blockchain. The referenced UTXOs are marked spent. The new outputs become fresh UTXOs belonging to the recipient.

7. Confirmations accumulate

Each new block mined on top of that block adds one confirmation. The more confirmations, the deeper the transaction is embedded in the blockchain, the more impossible it is to reverse.

PSBT: Partially Signed Bitcoin Transaction

The lifecycle above describes a transaction signed by one party. But what if a transaction needs to be signed by several parties, for example a 2-of-3 multisig, or if signing is done on a hardware wallet separate from the wallet that assembles the transaction?

PSBT (BIP 174) is a standard format for a transaction that is "in the process" of being signed. It separates two roles that were previously always combined: the role of the transaction assembler (who chooses the UTXOs, determines the outputs, and computes the fee) and the role of the signer (who has the private key). With PSBT, these two roles can be performed by different devices or parties.

The flow: the wallet software assembles the transaction and exports it as a PSBT file. This file contains all the information needed to sign: which inputs need to be signed, the script that must be satisfied, and additional metadata. A hardware wallet or another signer reads the PSBT file, verifies its details, adds its signature, and returns the partially signed PSBT. Once all signatures are collected, the PSBT is combined and finalized into a transaction ready to broadcast.

This entire process, from creation to the first confirmation, can finish in 10 minutes if the network isn’t busy and your fee is sufficient. No bank, no permission, no business hours, no holidays. Anywhere in the world, anytime.
