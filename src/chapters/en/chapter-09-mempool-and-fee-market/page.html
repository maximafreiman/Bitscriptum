Chapter 9 · 9.1
A waiting room with no center
What is the Mempool?

We met the mempool in 3.5 as one stage in a transaction’s journey. Now we stop there for longer, because the mempool is more than a waiting room: it is where the price of a transaction gets decided.

Every Bitcoin transaction that has been sent but not yet confirmed lives in a waiting room. This waiting room is called the mempool, short for memory pool. Every node has its own mempool, and there is no single "global mempool".

A waiting room with no center

Because every node manages its own mempool, two different nodes can have slightly different mempool contents. Node A might have received a transaction that hasn’t reached Node B yet. Node C might have already removed a transaction that’s still in Node D because of memory limits. This is normal and intentional. The mempool isn’t a synchronized global database, it’s a collection of transactions waiting for confirmation at each node independently.

What gets into the mempool?

Not all transactions are automatically accepted into the mempool. Every node has its own rules called mempool policy. Common rules that apply at most nodes: the transaction must be cryptographically valid, the UTXOs used must exist and be unspent, the fee must meet a minimum threshold, and the transaction size must be within a reasonable limit. If a transaction doesn’t meet the requirements, it’s rejected and doesn’t enter that node’s mempool.

How long can a transaction stay in the mempool?

A transaction that isn’t confirmed within a certain time is removed from the mempool. Most nodes remove transactions that are more than two weeks old. If a transaction is removed from the mempool before being confirmed, the funds aren’t lost. The UTXOs used return to their original state and the transaction can be rebroadcast.

The mempool and the fee market

The mempool is where the fee market forms naturally. Space in every block is limited. Miners choose which transactions to include in a block, and they have an economic incentive to choose the transactions with the highest fees. When the mempool is busy, users who want fast confirmation have to compete with higher fees.

The mempool is one of the most frequently misunderstood components of Bitcoin. Many people panic when they see their transaction stuck in the mempool. But as long as the transaction is valid and its fee is reasonable, it will be confirmed, whether now or later when the network is quieter.

Chapter 9 · 9.2
It’s not the biggest fee that wins, but the highest fee rate
Fee Rate and Priority

In the mempool, all transactions compete to get into the next block. But it’s not the transaction with the largest total fee that’s prioritized. What’s prioritized is the transaction with the highest fee rate.

What is fee rate?

Fee rate is the fee divided by the transaction size. Its unit is satoshi per virtual byte, written sat/vB. A transaction with a 1,000 satoshi fee and a size of 200 vbytes has a fee rate of 5 sat/vB. Another transaction with a 500 satoshi fee but a size of 50 vbytes has a fee rate of 10 sat/vB and will be prioritized higher, even though its total fee is smaller. This is logical from the miner’s point of view: a block has limited capacity in weight/vbytes, not in number of transactions. The miner wants to maximize revenue per unit of available space.

Virtual byte vs byte

Since SegWit was introduced, transaction size is measured in virtual bytes (vbytes) rather than ordinary bytes. Witness data (signatures) gets a 75% discount: every 4 bytes of witness counts as 1 vbyte. This is an incentive for users to use SegWit addresses because their transactions are lighter and their fee rate is more competitive for the same fee.

How do miners choose transactions?

Miners sort all transactions in the mempool by fee rate, from highest to lowest, then fill the block from the top until capacity runs out. In practice it’s more complex because there are dependencies between transactions: transaction B that uses the output of transaction A must come after A. But the basic principle holds: the highest fee rate means the highest priority.

What’s the right fee rate?

There’s no exact number. The right fee rate depends on how busy the mempool is at the time and how fast the transaction needs to be confirmed. When the mempool is quiet, even 1 sat/vB can be confirmed in the next block. When the mempool is very busy, fee rate can reach hundreds of sat/vB. Tools like mempool.space help you see the mempool conditions in real time.

Understanding fee rate is a basic skill every Bitcoin user needs. Too low and the transaction can be stuck for weeks. Too high and you pay more than you need to.

Chapter 9 · 9.3
Transaction stuck? There are two ways to fix it
RBF and CPFP

The transaction has been sent but its fee is too low and it’s stuck in the mempool. What can be done? There are two mechanisms that let a user speed up a transaction that’s already been sent: RBF and CPFP.

RBF: Replace-By-Fee

RBF lets the sender replace an unconfirmed transaction with a new version that has a higher fee. The new transaction uses the same UTXOs but pays a larger fee, so miners are more interested in including it in a block. To be replaceable, the original transaction must mark itself as replaceable by setting the sequence number to a certain value. This is called opt-in RBF. A node that receives the replacement transaction discards the old one and replaces it with the new, as long as the new transaction meets the requirements: a higher fee and a higher fee rate than the original.

Full RBF

There’s also full RBF, where a node accepts a transaction replacement even if the original transaction didn’t mark itself as replaceable. This is controversial because it touches the assumption that an unconfirmed transaction can be considered safe for small payments.

CPFP: Child Pays for Parent

CPFP is a different mechanism. If the original transaction (parent) is stuck because of a low fee, the recipient can create a new transaction (child) that uses the output of the parent transaction, with a very high fee. A miner who wants to take the fee from the child transaction has to include the parent transaction first because the child depends on the parent. Effectively, the high fee from the child subsidizes the confirmation of the low-fee parent. The miner calculates the combined fee rate of the parent+child package and compares it with other transactions in the mempool.

When to use RBF vs CPFP?

RBF is used by a sender who wants to replace their own transaction. CPFP can be used by a recipient, who can’t change a transaction that’s already been sent to them but can create a new transaction that pulls the parent into a block.

RBF and CPFP are proof that Bitcoin can be very flexible when it comes to fee management. Two different mechanisms to solve the same problem, with different trade-offs and assumptions. Understanding both makes users far more confident in managing their transactions.

The other side of that flexibility

Flexibility always has another side. The same rules that let you speed up your own transaction can be turned around to hold someone else’s in place. It is called transaction pinning.

The key is something we already covered in 9.1: the mempool is not part of consensus. It is simply each node’s policy about what it is willing to keep and relay. That policy has practical limits, such as how many descendant transactions may hang off a single unconfirmed one. Those limits exist to protect nodes from being flooded. But the same limits can be used to trap.

The shape of the attack is roughly this. The attacker creates a very large, very low-fee child transaction attached to the one they want to obstruct. The combination is too unattractive to mine, yet it has already filled the mempool policy quota. As a result the victim can no longer replace or accelerate their transaction, because the replacement is rejected by a policy that is already full. The transaction is not cancelled, it is simply pinned in place, stuck with no way to push it forward.

For an ordinary payment this is annoying. For Lightning it is dangerous. Recall from 7.2 that the penalty against cheating only works if the penalty transaction can reach the blockchain before the timelock expires. If an attacker can pin that transaction in the mempool until the deadline passes, the punishment mechanism that looked so solid in the simulation becomes powerless. The cryptography was not broken. The road to the blockchain was blocked.

This is a good illustration of two different layers of security. Chapter 4 dealt with security resting on mathematics, and mathematics does not negotiate. But at the mempool layer what governs is not mathematics but policy, and policy can always be exploited by someone who reads it more carefully than the people who wrote it.

So the fixes live at the policy layer too, not in consensus. One is package relay, which has nodes evaluate a transaction together with its descendants as a single package rather than one at a time, so that a low-fee transaction accompanied by a high-fee child is no longer turned away at the gate. Another is restricting the shape of transactions used by protocols like Lightning, so that the room an attacker has to attach a giant descendant becomes much narrower.

It is worth noting that fixes of this kind need no soft fork, because no consensus rule changes. Nodes simply update their policy. That makes them easier to deploy, but it also means the protection depends on how many nodes actually update.

Chapter 9 · 9.4
Why fees suddenly spike and what can be done
The Mempool When the Network is Busy

There are certain times when the Bitcoin mempool becomes very busy. Thousands of transactions pile up, fee rate spikes, and users paying low fees have to wait hours or even days. Understanding when and why this happens lets you anticipate and make better decisions.

What causes the mempool to get busy?

A busy mempool happens when the rate of incoming transactions exceeds the block capacity that can be confirmed. Every block has a capacity of about 4 million weight units. A weight unit is the formal unit of block capacity, and its relationship to the vbyte we used in 9.2 is simple: 1 vbyte equals 4 weight units. So 4 million weight units is the same as 1 million vbytes. Because witness data counts as a quarter, a single block can hold more raw bytes than that figure suggests, typically around 1.5 to 2.5 MB in practice. With an average block time of 10 minutes, the network generally processes about 3 to 7 transactions per second, depending on which transaction types dominate at the time. Some events that historically caused mempool spikes: the launch of Ordinals and Inscriptions in 2023, a bull market driving high trading activity, halving events that triggered massive speculation, and spam attacks that deliberately flooded the network with low-cost transactions.

What happens to low-fee transactions?

When the mempool is busy, miners can easily fill an entire block with only high-fee transactions. Low-fee transactions are pushed to the bottom of the queue and might not be confirmed for days. Some nodes will even drop low-fee transactions from their mempool to save memory, even though the transactions are valid.

How do you read mempool conditions?

There are several metrics that can be monitored. The mempool size in MB or the number of transactions shows how busy the queue is right now. The fee rate for confirmation within 1 block, 3 blocks, and 6 blocks shows how much you need to pay for different speeds. Tools like mempool.space display these visualizations in real time and are very helpful.

Strategies for dealing with a busy mempool

If it’s not urgent, wait until the mempool is quiet. Usually weekends or the middle of the night in Asia are quieter than peak hours. If you’ve already sent with a low fee, use RBF or CPFP. If you want to send a new transaction, monitor the mempool conditions first and use an accurate fee estimator.

A busy mempool isn’t a sign that Bitcoin is broken. It’s actually a sign that Bitcoin works exactly as designed: a free market that determines the price of block space based on demand. What you need to do is understand how it works and use it wisely.

Chapter 9 · 9.5
What’s the right fee for this situation?
Fee Estimation

Determining the right fee for a Bitcoin transaction isn’t an exact science. Too low and the transaction can be stuck. Too high and you waste money. Fee estimation is the process of estimating the minimum fee rate needed for a transaction to be confirmed within a certain target time.

How does a fee estimator work?

A good fee estimator analyzes the current mempool conditions and historical confirmation data to predict what fee rate is needed. There are two main approaches. The first is mempool-based: looking at the distribution of fee rates in the current mempool and estimating how many blocks are needed to clear the queue down to a certain fee level. The second is history-based: analyzing historical confirmation data to build a prediction model based on similar mempool conditions.

Confirmation target

Users can usually choose a confirmation target: next block (fastest, most expensive), 3 blocks (about 30 minutes), 6 blocks (about 1 hour), or even 1-2 days for non-urgent ones. The fee estimator gives the recommended fee rate for each target.

Limitations of fee estimators

Fee estimators have some important weaknesses. First, the mempool can change very fast so an accurate estimate from 5 minutes ago can already be irrelevant. Second, an estimator can’t predict a block that happens to be empty due to mining luck. Third, some wallets use a fee estimator that’s outdated or too conservative, so they always recommend a fee that’s too high.

Fee sniping

There’s also an interesting phenomenon called fee sniping: a situation where a miner might be more interested in re-mining a previous block that had high fees than mining a new block. This is addressed with a time-lock on transactions to ensure a transaction is only valid at a certain block height or higher.

Good fee estimation is a combination of real-time data and an understanding of how the mempool works. Tools like mempool.space provide the data needed, but it still takes human judgment to read the situation correctly, especially when mempool conditions are changing fast.

Chapter 9 · 9.6
When the block reward runs out, who pays the miners?
The Future of the Fee Market

Bitcoin is designed so that the block reward keeps decreasing with every halving. Eventually, around the year 2140, all 21 million bitcoin will have been mined and the block reward becomes zero. The only compensation for miners is transaction fees. This is one of the most fundamental questions about Bitcoin’s long-term sustainability.

Are fees enough to replace the block reward?

This is an open question with no definite answer yet. The optimistic camp argues that as Bitcoin adoption spreads, transaction volume will keep increasing. More transactions means more competition for block space, which means a higher fee rate. The Lightning Network and other layers don’t eliminate on-chain fees: every channel opening and closing still needs an on-chain transaction. The skeptical camp argues that the pressure for efficiency is always there. Transaction batching, SegWit, Taproot, and other technologies keep reducing transaction size. If fees are too low, miners will go out of business, hash rate drops, and network security is threatened.

Fee market and security

There’s a direct relationship between the fee market and network security that’s often misunderstood. Many people think: if miners leave, the difficulty adjustment will adjust, so the network stays secure. This is partly true, but misses the more important point.

The difficulty adjustment does ensure the network keeps functioning. If hash rate drops 50%, difficulty drops 50%, and block time returns to 10 minutes. Transactions keep being confirmed, the network doesn’t stop. What the difficulty adjustment doesn’t do is make an attack more expensive. Quite the opposite: if hash rate drops because miners aren’t profitable, the cost of launching a 51% attack drops proportionally too.

The analogy is simple: imagine a building’s security system. If half the guards are fired because the budget ran out, the building stays open and people can still enter. But that building is far easier to rob. The difficulty adjustment is a mechanism that ensures the building stays open, not a mechanism that maintains the number of guards. This is why a healthy fee market isn’t just about user convenience. It’s the foundation of long-term network security.

Not all fees last equally long

Not all demand for block space is equal in value over the long term. The healthiest demand comes from organic, widespread Bitcoin adoption: the more people transact, the more Lightning channels are opened and closed, the more institutions do on-chain settlement. High fees from these sources are a sign of a healthily growing ecosystem, and this is exactly the desired scenario: millions of ordinary users whose transaction needs create natural competition for block space.

Other sources are seasonal. The Ordinals and Inscriptions wave of 2023 we mentioned in 9.4 is the clearest example: fees spiked for months, then receded. For a security budget that has to hold for decades, spikes like that are hard to lean on, because nobody can guarantee the next wave will arrive.

This is where one of the most heated debates in recent Bitcoin history lives, and you deserve to hear both sides. One side argues that a transaction is a transaction: the Bitcoin protocol has never distinguished by purpose, whoever pays the fee has earned the block space, and miners get paid whether the data is a payment or a picture. The other side argues that block space is finite, and when it fills with things that do not need Bitcoin’s particular properties, people who do need to send money get pushed out by the fee spike.

This book will not decide for you. What you can hold onto as an analytical tool: what determines long-term security is not how high fees are today, but how reliably those fees come back next year and ten years from now. Every time you read news about a fee spike, the useful question is whether this is a wave or a rising tide.

Block space as a commodity

Bitcoin’s block space is a scarce commodity. There’s demand from various legitimate sources: financial transactions, Lightning channel management, layer-two protocols, and applications that provide real value. As long as meaningful demand exceeds capacity, the fee market will stay active organically.

The sustainability of Bitcoin’s fee market is one of the biggest economic experiments that has ever existed. What you can do is understand the incentives involved, distinguish healthy demand from unhealthy, and observe how the market responds over time.
