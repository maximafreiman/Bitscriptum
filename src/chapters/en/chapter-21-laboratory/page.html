Laboratory · Primitive C1
SHA-256, dissected from the inside

Most SHA-256 tools take text and spit out a hash. That teaches nothing. Here we open the pot: how your message gets wrapped, split into 64 words, then stirred 64 times until no trace of the original remains.

Step 1 · Your message gets wrapped first

SHA-256 can only work on chunks of exactly 512 bits. A message whose length is not a multiple of that has to be wrapped first, and the wrapping is not simply padding with zeros.

message bit 1 (80) padding length

Three parts, and the order is mandatory. First the original message. Then a single 1 bit as a boundary marker, which appears as 80 in bytes. Then as many zeros as needed. Then the final eight bytes hold the original message length in bits. Those last eight bytes are what guarantee that two messages of different lengths can never produce the same block, even if their contents are similar.

Step 2 · 16 words bred into 64

One 512-bit block is read as 16 words of 32 bits each. But there are 64 stirring rounds, so those 16 words must first be multiplied into 64.

Word 16 onward is not taken from the message. It is computed from four earlier words, two of which are rotated and shifted first. The effect compounds: word 63 already carries traces of nearly every word before it. This is the first reason a single flipped bit in the message can spread everywhere.

Step 3 · 64 rounds, but only two things change

This is the part where people usually give up. Eight registers named a through h, stirred 64 times. It looks complicated until you notice one thing.

Watch the column as you step. Six of the eight registers merely shift down: b becomes c, c becomes d, and so on. Nothing is computed for them, they simply queue. The only ones genuinely computed are a and e. So SHA-256 is not 64 complicated operations, it is one simple operation repeated 64 times over a queue that keeps moving.

Step 4 · Why one bit brings it all down

Now the part most often claimed and rarely shown. Two messages differing by a single bit, run side by side, counting how many bits differ at each round.

At round zero the number is zero, and that is not a miscount: the stirring has not begun, so all eight registers are still identical for both messages. What differs so far is a single bit in the message. Now advance the rounds. Within three rounds the difference has already passed a quarter, and around round twelve it reaches half and stays there to the end. Half is exactly the number we want, because it means the output is indistinguishable from random. That one bit did not spread gradually. It detonated.

Where do the magic numbers come from?
Final result

SHA-256 uses eight initial values and 64 constants. Those numbers were not chosen by anyone. The eight initial values are the fractional parts of the square roots of the first eight primes, and the 64 constants come from the cube roots of the first 64 primes. This is called nothing up my sleeve: anyone can recompute them and confirm no back door is hidden inside the numbers.

Laboratory · Primitive C2
secp256k1, dissected from zero

The formula is one line: P = k × G. But inside it sit clock arithmetic, a curve with finitely many points, and an addition that is not ordinary addition. We take it apart in order, and every number here can be checked on a calculator.

Step 1 · Before anything: what mod means

All arithmetic on Bitcoin’s curve wraps modulo a prime. If that term is unfamiliar, everything after it will feel like magic. So we start here.

Number

Mod is the remainder, nothing more. Picture a clock with only 17 numbers, 0 through 16. Count to 17 and you are back at 0. Count to 36 and you go round twice, stopping at 2. That is what 36 mod 17 = 2 means. The whole Bitcoin curve rests on an idea this simple, except its clock has about 10⁷⁷ numbers on it.

Step 2 · Check for yourself whether a point is on the curve

The curve has one equation: y² = x³ + 7. A point lies on it when both sides come out equal after wrapping mod 17. Do not take my word for it, compute it.

Move to a point that is not a member and watch the two sides disagree. This is the only membership condition. There is no secret list and no authority deciding: a point is on the curve if and only if the equation holds. Out of 289 possible coordinate pairs, only 17 pass.

Step 3 · The ladder: this is what k × G means

Now the heart of it. P = k × G is not ordinary multiplication. It means adding the point G to itself k times. Climb rung by rung, with the addition formula shown using the numbers currently in play.

Notice that point addition has its own formula, and it differs depending on whether the two points are the same. What gets computed first is always the slope of the line, λ. Once λ is known, the resulting coordinates follow from the last two lines. These are the exact formulas secp256k1 uses, only the numbers here are small. Notice too that the result always lands back on the curve, never outside it.

Step 4 · That ladder is too slow

Climbing rung by rung is fine for k = 17. For a k the size of Bitcoin’s, that ladder needs more steps than there are atoms in the universe. So a shortcut is used.

k in binary

The trick reads k in binary. Each bit means one doubling, and every bit set to 1 means one extra addition. The answer is identical to climbing the ladder, but the step count is not k, it is roughly twice the number of bits. For a 256-bit k that is about 384 steps, done in a fraction of a second.

Step 5 · So why is this called a key?

Every ingredient is on the table. The chain now reads as a single sentence.

Finding k from P by testing one at a time

It goes like this. k is your private key, one number only you know. P is the public key, the result of climbing the ladder k rungs, and anyone may see it. Computing forward from k to P is easy, and you just did it. Computing backward from P to k demands trying one at a time. On this toy curve, 17 attempts, which is exactly why this curve secures nothing. On secp256k1 the figure runs to 78 digits. Out of that asymmetry comes the ability to prove you know k without ever saying it, which is what C3 dissects.

Laboratory · Primitive C3
Signatures, dissected from the inside

In 4.3 you signed and verified. But what is actually inside a signature? Two numbers, r and s. Here we compute both by hand on the same toy curve from C2, then use them to pull a private key out of its owner. The curve is slightly larger than in C2 (modulo 43 rather than 17) because ECDSA requires the number of points to be prime.

Step 1 · Two numbers, and where they come from

An ECDSA signature is not a mysterious object. It is a pair of numbers. The first, r, comes from a random point on the curve. The second, s, binds that random point to your message and your private key.

Notice that k is never sent. It is used and discarded. The public key is not inside the signature either. All that travels is two numbers, and from those two numbers anyone can verify without ever learning the private key.

Step 2 · Verification, and why it lines up

Verification does not reverse the computation above. It takes a different route that, if every number is right, ends at the same point.

What gets compared is the x coordinate of the resulting point against the r that was sent. If they match, the signature holds. No step requires the private key, and no step leaks it. This is the asymmetry underneath all Bitcoin ownership: creating demands the secret, checking does not.

Step 3 · Reuse k, and it all falls apart

Now the part 4.3 could only warn about. We sign two different messages with the same k, then compute the private key from those two signatures. Not guessed. Computed.

Follow the chain. Because k is the same, r is the same too, and that is the tell anyone can spot on the blockchain. From there k is recovered through the difference, and once k is known the private key is one algebraic step away. No guessing anywhere. This is why modern wallets no longer randomise k but derive it deterministically from the private key and the message, as noted in 4.3.

Step 4 · Schnorr, and why it is tidier

Schnorr uses the same ingredients arranged differently. The change looks trivial in the formula, but its consequence is large.

ECDSA
R = k×G r = x(R) s = (z + r·d) / k
Schnorr
R = k×G e = hash(R, Q, z) s = k + e·d

In ECDSA, s contains a division by k. That division is what makes the formula non-linear, and that non-linearity is what blocks combining signatures. Schnorr replaces it with plain addition. Because everything is linear, two Schnorr signatures can be added into one that is still valid. That is the basis of the key aggregation you saw in 4.4, and the reason lives in this single operation.

Laboratory · Primitive C4
BIP32: one seed, millions of keys

Modern wallets do not store thousands of private keys. They store one seed and derive the rest on demand. Here the whole chain is computed for real and checked against the official BIP32 test vectors. At the end sits a demonstration of why that little apostrophe in a derivation path is not decoration.

Part 1 · From seed to master key

The seed from BIP39 goes into HMAC-SHA512, and the 64-byte result is split exactly down the middle.

Note the split. The left half becomes the master private key, the right half the chain code. A chain code is not a key and cannot spend anything, but it is required to derive children. Which is why leaking a chain code together with one public key is already dangerous, as part four will show.

Part 2 · Deriving a single child

A child is derived by feeding the chain code, the parent key, and an index back into HMAC-SHA512. Move the index and watch what changes.

The crux is the last line: the child key equals IL plus the parent key, added modulo n. That addition is what makes the whole system work, and it is also the weakness. Note too that each child gets its own chain code, so derivation can continue to grandchildren and beyond without limit.

Part 3 · Hardened: one small difference in the input

There are two ways to derive a child, and the difference is only in what goes into the HMAC. Compare the input rows in the two columns.

The ordinary form feeds in the parent public key, while the hardened form feeds in the parent private key. The consequence is large. Because the hardened form needs the private key, it cannot be derived from an xpub. That sounds like a limitation, but it is precisely the point, and the next part shows why.

Part 4 · Why hardened has to exist

Now the demonstration. Suppose someone holds your xpub, perhaps because you gave it to an accountant so they could watch the balance. Then one non-hardened child private key leaks. Press the button.

Look at what just happened. Because the child key equals IL plus the parent key, and IL can be computed by anyone holding the xpub, the parent key is simply a subtraction away: parent = child minus IL. One leaked child key brings down the entire wallet, including every other branch that never leaked. Against hardened derivation this attack is impossible, because its IL requires the very parent private key being hunted. That is why a path like m/84h/0h/0h uses apostrophes on its first three levels.

Reading a derivation path

Now a path like m/84h/0h/0h/0/0 can be read. m is the master key from the seed. 84h marks the address standard in use, here bc1q as in 6.4. The next 0h marks Bitcoin rather than another coin. The third 0h is the account number. All three of those are hardened, and now you know why. The last two carry no apostrophe: 0 means the receiving branch, and the final number is the address index. Both are deliberately non-hardened so that a single xpub can watch balances without being able to spend, as discussed in 2.3.

Laboratory · Primitive C5
BIP39: from random bits to twelve words

A seed phrase looks like a list of English words picked at random. In fact no word is picked at all. Every one of them comes from re-reading a string of bits, eleven bits per word, and one of those bit groups is not randomness but a checksum. Everything on this page is computed for real.

Part 1 · It all starts from random bits

What a wallet produces first is not words but a run of random bits. For a 12-word seed phrase, that run is 128 bits long.

Notice these 128 bits are not the result of any computation. They are pure randomness, exactly as covered in 2.4. Everything that follows only changes how they are read; nothing new is added.

Part 2 · The checksum: four bits from SHA-256

Before being cut into words, the bit string gets a checksum appended. Its source is the hash function you already dissected in C1.

The rule is simple: the checksum length equals the entropy length divided by 32. For 128 bits of entropy that means 4 checksum bits; for 256 bits, 8. Those bits are taken from the very front of the SHA-256 output and the rest discarded. Once appended the total becomes 132 bits, and that number matters: 132 divides evenly by 11.

Part 3 · Cut eleven bits, get a word

Now those 132 bits are cut into twelve groups of eleven bits each. Each group is read as a number, and that number is an index into the official word list.

IndexEleven bits #Word

Why eleven bits? Because two to the eleventh is 2048, and the BIP39 word list holds exactly 2048 words. Not a coincidence; it was designed that way. Note the final group too: some of its bits come from the entropy and some from the checksum. So the last word is not fully random, and that is what makes typos catchable.

Part 4 · Change one word and see what happens

Because the last word carries the checksum, altering any word usually makes the whole phrase invalid. Try it.

Word number

The chance that a random change happens to remain valid is only about one in sixteen for a 12-word seed, because its checksum is four bits. Small, but not zero, so the BIP39 checksum is a safety net against typos, not an absolute guarantee. For a 24-word seed the checksum is eight bits, dropping the odds to one in 256. And a small useful detail: the first four letters of every word on the list are unique, so typing four letters is actually enough.

One thing worth correcting

A seed phrase is often described as a "translation" of the private key. That is backwards. What comes first is the entropy, and from it both the seed phrase and the private key are derived, as already corrected in 2.4. Nor are the words an encryption of a secret: anyone who knows those twelve words can recompute your entire wallet, because the word list is public and so are the rules. The words are not a locked key. They are simply a way of writing bits down that a human can copy without error.

Laboratory · Primitive C6
The Taproot tweak: binding rules into a point

Chapter 6.6 explains that Taproot has two spending paths but never shows how both can live inside the same point. The answer is a single addition, and one algebraic identity that makes it all work. The curve is the same as in C2, so every number is checkable.

Part 1 · The internal key, before anything is done to it

It all starts from an ordinary key pair, exactly as in C2. There is no Taproot here yet.

This point P is called the internal key. If we stopped here it would just be a normal public key. What makes it Taproot is the next step.

Part 2 · The tweak: attaching a script tree

Now the backup spending rules, in the form of a merkle root over a script tree, are attached to that key. Not stored beside it, but fused into it.

Note the order. The number t is computed by hashing P together with the merkle root, so it is bound to both at once: change the script and t changes; change the key and t changes too. Then Q = P + t×G, and Q is what appears on the blockchain as a bc1p address. Note too that the merkle root is nowhere visible in Q. All that is recorded is a single point, and from that point nobody can guess what rules sit behind it, or even whether any rules exist at all.

Part 3 · The identity that makes it all work

There is one property, easy to miss, that carries the entire Taproot design. We compute two different routes and compare the results.

Both routes always end at the same point, and that is no accident. Since P equals d×G, it follows that P + t×G equals (d+t)×G. Which means the key owner needs no special treatment at all: they simply sign using d + t as their new private key, and the signature will be valid against Q. No script is executed, no tree is opened, no proof is attached. This is the key path spend.

Part 4 · Two paths, one point

Now the comparison that 6.6 describes in words, shown here through what the transaction actually contains.

Signature only

~64 byte

The size difference is not merely a cost saving. Because a key path spend contains only one signature, it looks exactly like an ordinary payment from any Taproot address. An observer cannot tell whether a script tree holding multisig or timelocks sits behind that key, or nothing at all. The complex rules still exist and remain usable, but as long as everyone cooperates they never touch the blockchain. This is why Taproot is called a privacy gain, not just a size one.

An honest note about this toy curve

Everything on this page uses modulo 17 with order 18 so the numbers stay followable. Two things differ on real secp256k1. First, t is computed with a tagged hash, a SHA-256 prefixed twice with the marker "TapTweak", so that hashes for different purposes can never be confused. Second, its order is an enormous prime, making the d+t equals zero case that occasionally appears here practically impossible. Otherwise the formulas and the flow are identical.

Laboratory · Primitive C7
Merkle: the odd rule and the flaw it produced

This book already has two Merkle demonstrations, in 4.5 and 17.2. What neither has ever mentioned: what happens when a level turns out to hold an odd number of nodes. The answer is one simple rule, and that rule once produced a real flaw that came close to halting the network.

Part 1 · When the count is odd

A Merkle tree pairs nodes two at a time. But the number of transactions in a block is not always even. Move the count and watch the odd levels.

The rule: when a level holds an odd count, the last node is duplicated and paired with itself. Not padded with zeros, not carried up alone. Duplicated. It is a design decision that looks reasonable and harmless, and for three years it was.

Part 2 · The flaw that rule produced

Look carefully. If the last node is duplicated, then a list of three items is treated exactly like a list of four whose final two are twins.

Two different transaction lists produce an identical merkle root. This is not a hash collision; SHA-256 is not broken in the slightest. What is flawed is the tree construction rule. It is catalogued as CVE-2012-2459, and its consequence was dangerous: an attacker could take a valid block, duplicate its final transaction, and broadcast that forgery. A node receiving it would reject it, because duplicated transactions are plainly invalid, but since the merkle root matched the real one, the node would mark the genuine block as permanently invalid and refuse the correct version. The network could split without a single hash being broken.

Part 3 · How it was patched
Before the patch

A block with duplicated transactions is accepted, then rejected for invalid contents

After the patch

A block with duplicated transactions is rejected first, before the root is computed

The patch did not change how the tree is built, because that would have been a hard fork invalidating every old block. What was done is simpler: nodes now reject any block containing exactly duplicated transactions, before the merkle root is ever computed. The flaw still sits inside the tree design, but the road to it is closed. This is the same pattern as CVE-2018-17144 in 14.1: when a consensus rule cannot be changed, what changes is what gets accepted before that rule runs. And as with that incident, the lesson is not about the cryptography but about assumptions nobody wrote down. Nobody ever wrote that two different lists must yield different roots. It was simply taken for granted.

Halving and DCA, worked out yourself

The previous three groups answer how Bitcoin works. This one answers a different question: what it means for someone holding it. Everything on this page is pure arithmetic, no price data is fetched from anywhere, and nothing here forecasts.

Part 1 · Thinking in satoshis

One bitcoin splits into 100 million satoshis. That number feels large until you set it beside the number of people.

Look at the last figure. If all 21 million bitcoin were split evenly among everyone on earth, each person would get roughly 262 thousand satoshis, or 0.0026 BTC. That is the ceiling, and it assumes none were ever lost. Millions already are, as covered in 11.2. Thinking in satoshis turns the question from "how many bitcoin can I own" into "what share of what will ever exist".

Part 2 · Issuance rate, and what each halving changes

Roughly every four years, the new bitcoin per block is cut in half. What people rarely work out is the effect on the daily rate.

Halving eraReward per block New bitcoin per dayApproximate year Total in circulationLeft to be mined
144 blocks per day, on average

That daily figure is the one seldom mentioned. At every halving, the amount of new bitcoin entering the world each day is halved, and it happens in a single block, not gradually. Look at the last column too: as time passes, less and less remains to be mined. This whole schedule was fixed in 2009 and depends on nothing, price included.

Part 3 · The mechanics of DCA, not its promise

DCA means buying the same amount of money regularly, whatever the price. What is interesting is not the outcome but the mechanism, and that can be worked out without knowing any future price.

PeriodPrice Satoshis received

This is the entire mechanism: because the money amount is fixed, a lower price automatically buys more satoshis. So your average purchase price is always lower than or equal to the average price over the period, never higher. That is a mathematical property, not a forecast. What nobody promises: whether the price rises at all. DCA reduces the risk of bad timing; it does not remove price risk.

Part 4 · Why your average is always lower

Part 3 showed the numbers. This part shows why it is not a coincidence, and why it holds for any price pattern.

Your average purchase price is total money divided by total satoshis, which is the harmonic mean. The market average is the ordinary mean of each period’s price. In mathematics, the harmonic mean is never greater than the ordinary mean, and the two are equal only when every price is identical. That is why the difference column above is never negative, whatever pattern you choose. Try the flat pattern and watch it drop to zero.

This page is a calculator, not financial advice. It does not forecast prices, does not recommend buying, and knows nothing about market conditions. Every number here comes from what you enter.

Address type distribution, and two contradictory stories

Chapter 6 taught five address types and how they work. What it never answered: which ones the world actually uses. The answer turns out to depend on how you count, and the two ways of counting lead to opposite conclusions.

Part 1 · Counting by UTXO

The usual way to measure the distribution is to count how many UTXOs exist of each type. Here is the result.

First surprise: Taproot leads, despite being the newest and only active since 2021. P2PKH, present since the genesis block, sits second. And P2PK, the earliest format, the one Satoshi himself used, has practically vanished at 0.03 percent. But hold off on concluding anything, because this way of counting hides something.

Part 2 · Counting by value, the order reverses

Now count differently: how much bitcoin actually sits in each type. Two figures below are enough to demolish the conclusion above.

Look at those two rows carefully. Taproot holds a third of all UTXOs but under one percent of the value. And P2PK holds three ten-thousandths of the UTXOs but nearly a tenth of all value. Divide it out: one average P2PK UTXO carries roughly 13 thousand times more bitcoin than one Taproot UTXO. Both anomalies have their own cause, and both are interesting.

The answer is not about wallet adoption. It is about what is being stored there.

Nearly 30 percent of all Bitcoin UTXOs relate to inscriptions, most of them using Taproot. Yet all 51 million of those UTXOs together hold only about 415 BTC, averaging 811 satoshis each. That is dust. So the 34 percent figure is not a sign that Taproot won as a payment format, but a sign that it is being used for something else. Whether that is legitimate use we present both sides of in 9.6. What is not disputed: the UTXO set grew considerably because of it, and every node must store it, as discussed in 10.1.

Part 4 · Why P2PK is scarce yet heavy

This one runs the other way: very few of them, holding an enormous amount of value.

P2PK is the earliest format, used for mining rewards in the first years, including Satoshi’s own. Almost all of them have never been spent since creation, and most are presumed lost forever, as covered in 11.2. But something else makes them significant, and it connects to 4.6: P2PK places the raw public key directly in the output, never hidden behind a hash. Which means all of that value has been exposed since day one, and nobody can move it if the keys really are gone.

A fair question: if newer formats are cheaper and more private, why does P2PKH from 2009 still hold nearly a third of all UTXOs? Three reasons. First, old UTXOs do not migrate on their own: they only change type when their owner spends them, and many have never moved. Second, some older services have not upgraded. Third, sending to a newer format requires the counterparty to support it too, and that is a change needing both sides. So the chart above is not a photograph of what people choose today. It is sediment from fifteen years of decisions piling on each other.

Laboratory · Format F1
Transaction serialization, byte by byte

A Bitcoin transaction travelling the network is not an object, not JSON, and not a table. It is a sequence of bytes in a fixed order. Here we dissect one whole transaction, then use it to answer something the book has only alluded to: why SegWit put an end to malleability.

Part 1 · Reading the byte sequence

Click any coloured chunk to see what it holds. The field order is fixed, and there are no name tags anywhere: a parser knows what it is reading purely because it knows the order.

Notice the two things that confuse people most. First, the input txid is written backwards. The first byte on screen is the last byte of the txid you see in a block explorer. Second, the 00 marker and 01 flag in third position. In an old-style transaction that position holds the input count, and an input count can never be zero. That very impossibility is what SegWit uses as its signal: old parsers read it as a transaction with no inputs and stop, new parsers recognise a flag.

Part 2 · Varint, the thing that trips people up

Numbers inside a transaction are not fixed width. Small numbers compress into one byte, larger ones get a prefix marker. This is what makes people miscount when reading raw hex.

NumberEncoded asSize

The rule is only four lines, but a parser must know it cold: below 253, one plain byte. From 253 up, the first byte becomes a length marker, not a value. So fd means "the next two bytes are the number", not the number 253. Misread a single varint and the entire rest of the transaction is misread too, because every position shifts.

Part 3 · txid and wtxid, and where they part ways

One SegWit transaction has two identities. Both are double SHA-256 hashes, but over different stretches of bytes.

Here is the split. txid is computed from the serialization without marker, flag, and witness. wtxid is computed from the complete serialization. Which means the entire signature sits outside the reach of txid. Press the button below and see the consequence yourself.

Part 4 · Malleability, demonstrated rather than described

Sections 6.4 and 13.4 name malleability as the problem SegWit solved, but never show it. Now we flip a single bit of the signature in both formats and compare.

The result speaks for itself. In the old format the signature lives inside scriptSig, and scriptSig counts toward the txid. So anyone relaying your transaction could alter the signature encoding without invalidating it, and the txid changes. Any transaction waiting on that txid is left dangling. Under SegWit the signature moved into the witness, outside the txid, so the txid does not budge. This is not a patch; it is the direct consequence of moving one piece of data out of the hash’s reach. Lightning in Chapter 7 depends entirely on txids that cannot be shifted like this.

Laboratory · Format F2
Address checksums, computed in front of you

A Bitcoin address is not simply a polished hash. It is wrapped in a checksum so that typos are caught before funds are sent. Two formats use two entirely different methods, and the difference is not about which is stricter but about what each can prove.

Part 1 · base58check: using SHA-256 twice

The older format builds its checksum from the hash function you already dissected in C1. Four steps, all of which you can follow.

Notice the checksum is only the first four bytes of the double hash. The rest is discarded. Four bytes means 32 bits, so the chance that a typo happens to produce the same checksum is roughly one in four billion. Small, but that is a probability, not a guarantee. Notice the alphabet too: base58 deliberately drops the digit 0, the letter O, the letter I, and the letter l, because all four are easily confused by a human reader.

Part 2 · bech32: no hashing at all

The newer format does not use SHA-256. It uses an error-correcting code, from the same branch of mathematics behind CDs and QR codes.

Those last six characters are the checksum, and they come from a polynomial computation rather than a hash. That is why it can prove something a hash cannot: as long as no more than four characters are wrong, detection is guaranteed. Not almost certain, but certain. Notice the bc at the front too: that is the network marker, and it is fed into the checksum, so a mainnet address can never pass as a testnet one.

Part 3 · Break one character

Change a single character in either address and see whether it is caught. Try a few different positions.

base58check
bech32

You will find that both catch single-character errors, and that is genuinely true. I tested four thousand random alterations on each, and not one slipped through. So anyone claiming bech32 is more typo-resistant is not right for the single-character case. The difference lies elsewhere: base58check almost certainly catches it because collisions are so unlikely, while bech32 is mathematically guaranteed to catch it as long as four or fewer characters are wrong. One rests on probability, the other on proof.

Part 4 · When the standard itself turned out to be flawed

There is a part of this story rarely told, and it fits the spirit of Chapter 14.

FormatFinal constantUsed for
bech321SegWit v0 (bc1q)
bech32m0x2bc830a3SegWit v1 and up (bc1p, Taproot)

Bech32 shipped in 2017 and saw wide use. In 2020 it emerged that the constant at the end of its computation had a flaw: under certain conditions, adding or removing a character at the end was not always detected. For SegWit version zero addresses the consequence was harmless, because their length is fixed. But for later versions, Taproot included, it mattered. The answer was bech32m, identical in every respect except one constant on the final line. That is why bc1q and bc1p addresses look alike yet are validated against different constants, and why a wallet that has not updated can reject a perfectly valid Taproot address.

Laboratory · Format F3
nBits: four bytes holding a 256-bit number

Chapter 5.4 covers difficulty adjustment at length but never shows where the target is actually stored. The answer is four bytes inside the block header, and the way four bytes hold a 256-bit number is both simpler and more misleading than most people assume.

Part 1 · Take the four bytes apart

The first byte is called the exponent, the other three the mantissa. Move the values and watch the resulting target.

exponent mantissa 
Target (32 bytes)

Here is the trap people fall into most often: the exponent is not a power of two. It is the length of the target in bytes. So an exponent of 29 means the target is 29 bytes long, with the mantissa placed in the three leading bytes of those 29. The rest are all zero. That is why targets always have the same shape: a few meaningful digits up front, then a long run of zeros.

Part 2 · Its history in five numbers

Real nBits values from various eras. Watch how the exponent shrinks over time.

EranBits ExpDifficulty

An exponent shrinking from 29 to 23 means the target grows shorter, and a shorter target means fewer hashes satisfy it. That is rising difficulty, seen from the storage side. And note the scale of the jump: from difficulty 1 at genesis to tens of trillions, purely by changing four bytes.

Part 3 · Why compress it at all

A fair question: why not simply store the full 32-byte target? Because the block header is fixed at exactly 80 bytes, and every byte in it is hashed millions of times per second by every miner on earth. Storing the full target would make the header 108 bytes, a 35 percent increase, for information that is almost entirely zeros. This compression has a price too: because the mantissa is only three bytes, not every 256-bit number can be expressed. The real target is always rounded to the nearest representable form. Precision is sacrificed, and that is fine, because what is needed is a threshold, not an exact figure.

Part 4 · Try it in reverse: target to nBits

Now the other direction. Enter how many leading zeros you want, and see the nBits that represents it.

Notice that adding one leading zero shrinks the target sixteenfold, because a single hex digit is worth four bits. So every extra zero you see at the front of a block hash represents sixteen times more work. This is the numerical account of something explained only in words in 5.2 and 5.4.
