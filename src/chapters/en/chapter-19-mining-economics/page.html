Chapter 19 · 19.1
A miner is paid by two sources with very different characters — and the proportion will change drastically over time
Anatomy of Miner Revenue — Block Reward, Fee, and Coinbase Transaction

Before talking about mining economics, we first need to understand where a miner’s money actually comes from. The answer is simple but often misunderstood: a miner gets two sources of income with very different characters.

Block reward: bitcoin minted by the protocol

The block reward is new bitcoin created in each block according to a schedule fixed since the genesis block. This isn’t money taken from anyone, but genuinely newly minted by the protocol. Currently the block reward is 3.125 BTC per block. This amount is halved every 210,000 blocks (about 4 years) in a process called the halving.

Transaction fees: a fluctuating open market

The second source of income is transaction fees from all the transactions included in a block. A fee is the difference between the total input and total output of a transaction, and what "disappears" from the UTXO set automatically becomes the property of the miner who includes that transaction in a block. Unlike the block reward, which is fixed and predictable, fees are very fluctuating. Transaction senders compete to get into the next block by offering a fee high enough to attract a miner’s interest.

Coinbase transaction: the only way new bitcoin is created

The block reward is claimed by the miner through the coinbase transaction, the first transaction in every block that has no input from any UTXO. This is the only transaction that can create new bitcoin, and it’s valid under the consensus rules. The coinbase output can’t be spent for the next 100 blocks, known as the coinbase maturity rule, to protect the ecosystem from a situation where a block is orphaned after the miner has already spent the reward.

A proportion that changes over time

Currently the block reward dominates miner income, about 95-99% of total revenue. This will change with the next halvings. Eventually, according to the schedule fixed since the genesis block, the block reward will reach zero around the year 2140 and miners will depend entirely on fees. Whether fees alone are enough to secure the network is one of the most important open questions in Bitcoin economics.

Why this matters for network security

Miner revenue is what pays for hashrate. High hashrate means a 51% attack becomes very expensive. If miner revenue drops because the Bitcoin price falls or the block reward shrinks without a sufficient rise in fees, hashrate can drop and the network becomes more vulnerable. Understanding where a miner’s money comes from is the foundation for understanding Bitcoin’s economic security.

These two sources of miner revenue aren’t a coincidence, but a deliberate design. The block reward ensures miners have a strong incentive early on when transactions are still few. Fees ensure the incentive remains after the block reward runs out. The big question is whether the transition from block reward to fees as the main source can go smoothly without sacrificing network security.

Chapter 19 · 19.2
No matter how many or how few miners are active, Bitcoin always finds one block every 10 minutes — how does this happen?
Difficulty Adjustment — How Bitcoin Maintains 10 Minutes

We covered how difficulty adjustment works back in 5.4. There the question was technical: what the mechanism does. Here the question is economic: what it does to a miner’s money, and why this is the mechanism that keeps mining from ever truly dying out.

One of the most elegant mechanisms in Bitcoin is difficulty adjustment: a system that automatically adjusts how hard it is to find a new block, so that on average one block is found every 10 minutes, no matter how many or how few miners are active.

Why 10 minutes?

Ten minutes is a deliberate trade-off. Too fast means a new block is found before the previous block has had time to spread across the network, increasing the risk of accidental forks and giving an advantage to miners with the fastest connections. Too slow means the first confirmation of a transaction takes too long. Ten minutes gives enough time for block propagation across the network while still providing reasonably fast confirmation.

The difficulty adjustment mechanism

Every 2,016 blocks (about 2 weeks if the average stays at 10 minutes), the entire network automatically recalculates the difficulty target. The method: compute how long the last 2,016 blocks took, compare it with the target of 20,160 minutes (the ideal 2 weeks), then adjust proportionally.

If the last 2,016 blocks were completed in 7 days because hashrate rose, difficulty is raised so blocks again take 10 minutes on average. If they were completed in 28 days because hashrate fell, difficulty is lowered. There’s an important limit: difficulty can’t rise or fall more than 4x in a single adjustment, to prevent extreme changes that could destabilize the network.

The difficulty target as a number

The difficulty target is a 256-bit number that must be greater than the hash of a valid block. The smaller the target number, the harder it is to find a valid hash because the miner has to try more combinations before finding a hash small enough. There’s no central server computing the difficulty, because every node computes it itself from the same blockchain data and, because the process is deterministic, all nodes get an identical number.

Economic implications

Difficulty adjustment makes Bitcoin self-regulating on the production side. If the Bitcoin price rises and more miners join, difficulty rises and the cost per block rises too until profitability returns to equilibrium. If the price falls and miners leave, difficulty falls so mining becomes economical again for the remaining miners. This is also what makes Bitcoin’s emission schedule predictable: even if hashrate changes drastically, the halving still occurs every ~210,000 blocks because difficulty adjustment maintains 10 minutes per block.

Difficulty adjustment is one of Satoshi’s most important innovations that often doesn’t get enough attention. Without it, Bitcoin couldn’t function as a predictable system: the emission schedule could be chaotic, block propagation could be inconsistent, and the entire consensus mechanism could break. The simplicity of its formula hides how fundamental the role it plays is.

Bitcoin’s difficulty adjustment formula is simple but powerful. There’s one important restriction: the change can’t be more than 4× in a single epoch.
// Difficulty Adjustment Formula
// Run every 2,016 blocks by every node independently

target_time = 2016 × 10 // 20,160 minutes = ~14 days
actual_time = timestamp[2016] - timestamp[0] // in minutes

ratio = actual_time / target_time

// Limit: the ratio can’t be more than 4× or less than 1/4×
ratio = max(0.25, min(4.0, ratio))

new_target = old_target × ratio
// Larger target = easier difficulty
// Smaller target = harder difficulty
Why does the 4× limit matter?
Without a limit, a very large hashrate surge could make difficulty rise too drastically in one epoch. The 4× limit ensures gradual stabilization — at most a few epochs to return to equilibrium even with a very extreme hashrate change.
Decentralized calculation
There’s no central server computing the difficulty. Every node computes it itself from the same blockchain data and, because it’s deterministic, all nodes get an identical number. A block with the wrong difficulty is rejected by all nodes.
Implementation detail: off-by-one
The timestamps compared are between the first and last block of each epoch, not the genesis block. The genesis block (#0) has a hardcoded timestamp so the first epoch causes no problem. This is an example of how real implementations often have details not present in the high-level description.
Chapter 19 · 19.3
Every 210,000 blocks, the block reward is cut in half — and this brings the most important open question in long-term Bitcoin economics
Halving & Security Budget — What Happens When the Block Reward Approaches Zero?

The halving is one of the most fundamental mechanisms in Bitcoin: every 210,000 blocks, the block reward is cut exactly in half. This isn’t a decision made by anyone, but happens automatically according to rules embedded in the protocol since the genesis block.

The halving schedule is already certain

First halving at block #210,000 (November 2012): the block reward dropped from 50 BTC to 25 BTC. Second halving at block #420,000 (July 2016): dropped to 12.5 BTC. Third halving at block #630,000 (May 2020): dropped to 6.25 BTC. Fourth halving at block #840,000 (April 2024): dropped to 3.125 BTC. The total bitcoin that can ever exist is 21 million, the result of summing a geometric series: 210,000 blocks × 50 BTC × (1 + 0.5 + 0.25 + ...) = 21 million BTC.

Security budget: the key concept

The security budget is the total miner revenue in a given time period. It’s an economic measure of how expensive an attack on the network is: the higher the security budget, the more hashrate miners can buy, and the more expensive it is for an attacker to gather enough hashrate for a 51% attack. The security budget consists of the block reward in BTC multiplied by the BTC price at the time, plus the total fees collected. Currently the block reward dominates about 95-99% of the total security budget.

The open question: are fees alone enough?

When the block reward approaches zero in the next century, miners will depend entirely on fees. Whether fees will be enough to maintain high hashrate is one of the most important and genuinely unanswered questions in Bitcoin economics. Some argue that as adoption grows, transaction volume and total fees per block will naturally increase. Others argue that the fee per transaction may not be high enough to replace the block reward, especially if layer-2 solutions take many transactions away from layer-1. No one can answer this with certainty today because it depends on many unpredictable variables.

What can be known now

The proportion of fees in the total security budget slowly rises with each halving, not because fees spike drastically, but because the block reward keeps shrinking. Whether this trend will continue at a sufficient pace is a question only time and future data can answer.

The block reward will approach zero around the year 2140, more than 100 years from now. Many aspects of the Bitcoin ecosystem in 2140 can’t be predicted from 2025. What’s certain: the halving mechanism is a consensus rule that can’t be changed without the agreement of the entire network, and the question of the long-term security budget will remain relevant with each subsequent halving.

Chapter 19 · 19.4
Solo mining is nearly impossible for a small miner today — pools exist to solve the variance problem, not to reduce total income
Mining Pool Economics — Why Do Miners Join a Pool?

Solo mining Bitcoin today is statistically almost impossible for a small miner. With network hashrate reaching hundreds of exahash per second, a miner with one modern ASIC producing 100 terahash per second has a probability of finding a block once every few hundred years. Mining pools solve this problem by combining the hashrate of many miners and splitting the reward proportionally.

How a mining pool works

The pool operator runs a full node and prepares the block template. Miners who join receive work from the pool: they’re asked to find a hash that meets a target far easier than the actual network target. A hash that meets this easier target is called a share, as proof that the miner is genuinely working. When one of the miners finds a hash that meets the actual network target, the pool gets the block reward and distributes it to all members based on how many shares each contributed.

Payment schemes: PPS, PPLNS, and FPPS

PPS (Pay Per Share) pays a miner for every valid share, regardless of whether the pool actually finds a block. The pool bears all the variance risk. More stable for the miner but the pool usually takes a higher fee. PPLNS (Pay Per Last N Shares) pays a miner based on the shares contributed within a certain window before a block is found. The miner shares the variance, the pool fee is lower, and it’s more profitable for consistent miners. FPPS (Full Pay Per Share) is like PPS but includes an estimate of transaction fees, not just the block reward.

Pool fee and its implications

The pool takes a small percentage of the total reward as an operational fee, usually 1-3%. The long-term total income between solo mining and pool mining is theoretically the same if the hashrate is the same, but a pool provides far more consistent and predictable income because of the far lower variance.

Implications for decentralization

Mining pools improve the efficiency and income stability of small miners, but the pool operator who manages the block template has influence over which transactions get included in a block. Miners who join a pool delegate this decision to the pool operator. The concentration of hashrate in large pools is something the community watches from the perspective of network decentralization.

A pool doesn’t increase miners’ total income in aggregate, the total reward from the network stays the same. What a pool does is redistribute the reward from an irregular pattern (solo) into a consistent, predictable stream. This is a trade-off between variance and a slight centralization at the block template level.

Chapter 19 · 19.5
Does a rational miner always follow the protocol? Selfish mining shows that under certain conditions, the answer is no
Selfish Mining & Miner Behavior — When Does a Rational Miner Deviate from the Protocol?

Bitcoin’s basic assumption is that a rational miner will always follow the protocol because that’s what’s most profitable. But is this always true? Selfish mining is a strategy that shows that under certain conditions, a miner can gain more by deviating from the honest mining protocol.

Honest mining vs selfish mining

Honest mining is the default strategy: when a miner finds a new block, they immediately propagate it to the entire network. All miners start working on top of the latest block simultaneously and the probability of getting a reward is proportional to each one’s hashrate. Selfish mining, first formally analyzed by Eyal and Sirer in 2013, is a strategy in which a miner with significant hashrate hides a newly found block instead of immediately propagating it. The miner keeps working secretly on top of this hidden block while the rest of the network is still working on the old block.

Why selfish mining can be profitable

If the selfish miner can find a second block before the network finds one block, they have a two-block lead. When the longer chain is finally published, all the work of the rest of the network is orphaned and the selfish miner gets more than their proportional share. Eyal and Sirer’s analysis shows that selfish mining can be profitable if a miner has more than about 33% of the network hashrate, depending on network connectivity.

Mitigation: fast propagation shifts the threshold

The faster a block spreads across the network, the smaller the window for a selfish miner to exploit their lead. Compact blocks and fast propagation technologies like FIBRE directly raise the hashrate threshold needed for selfish mining to be profitable. Besides that, unusual block patterns can be detected by nodes and researchers monitoring the network, and a large pool caught doing selfish mining can lose the trust of the miners who join it.

Fee sniping: another form of miner behavior

Fee sniping is another scenario: a miner with large hashrate can try to reorganize the last block to grab a very high transaction fee in that block. This is more relevant in the future when fees become the dominant component of miner revenue. Bitcoin Core has already implemented a default locktime to reduce the incentive for fee sniping by making transactions invalid in blocks before a certain height.

Selfish mining isn’t just academic theory, but a reminder that Bitcoin’s incentive design must keep being analyzed as network conditions change. The larger the proportion of fees in total revenue, the more important it is to understand how miner incentives can shift. What keeps Bitcoin healthy isn’t just cryptography, but the economic design that makes honest behavior the most rational strategy.

Chapter 19 · 19.6
Bitcoin mining is a global industry that follows cheap energy, depends on highly specialized hardware, and operates through a protocol that determines who controls the contents of a block
The Mining Ecosystem — Hardware, Energy, and Protocol

Bitcoin mining in the real world is an industry very different from the simple picture of "computers guessing numbers." It involves specialized hardware designed for one purpose, highly economic energy decisions, geographic distribution that follows electricity prices, and a communication protocol that determines who has control over block content.

ASIC: hardware born from one purpose

An ASIC (Application-Specific Integrated Circuit) is a chip designed specifically to run one operation: the SHA256 double hash as fast as possible with as little energy as possible. The key efficiency metric of an ASIC is joules per terahash (J/TH): how much energy is needed to produce one terahash. The first generation of Bitcoin ASICs around 2013 needed more than 1,000 J/TH. The best modern hardware today is already in the range of 15-20 J/TH, an efficiency improvement of more than 50-fold in a decade. Miners with old hardware are economically forced out when the Bitcoin price falls or difficulty rises, because the electricity cost exceeds the income.

Energy and geographic distribution

Bitcoin mining is a competition in energy efficiency. The miners who survive are those who can get the cheapest electricity: hydro plants with excess capacity, geothermal energy, flare gas from oil fields, or solar energy in remote areas. The global hashrate distribution changes significantly with regulation. After China banned mining in 2021, the hashrate that was once dominant in China migrated to the United States, Kazakhstan, Russia, and elsewhere within months. This shows that mining is very geographically mobile and the Bitcoin network is resistant to local regulation because hashrate can move to another jurisdiction.

The Stratum protocol: who controls the block template

Stratum is the communication protocol between the pool server and the mining hardware. In Stratum v1, still dominant today, the pool operator assembles the block template and decides which transactions are included. The miner only receives work in the form of a header to hash, without control over the block content. Stratum v2 introduces job negotiation: an individual miner can choose their own transactions and only ask the pool to validate their work. This significantly improves decentralization because control of the block template is no longer centralized in the pool operator.

These three aspects are interconnected: hardware efficiency determines where a miner can operate economically, geographic distribution determines the network’s resistance to regulation, and the communication protocol determines how decentralized the decisions about block content are. Understanding all three together gives a far more complete picture of how Bitcoin mining really works in the real world.
