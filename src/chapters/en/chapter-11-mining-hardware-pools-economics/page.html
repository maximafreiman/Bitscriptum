Chapter 11 · 11.1
In Chapter 5 we learned why mining exists — now we see how the industry works
From Protocol to Industry

In Chapter 5 we already understood why mining exists: Proof of Work is the consensus mechanism that allows Bitcoin to reach agreement without a central authority. Every valid block represents real work that has been done, and that real work requires real energy.

In this chapter we won’t repeat that mechanism. What we’ll discuss is how mining operates as a real industry: what kind of hardware is used, how miners collaborate in pools, how the economics work, and what the implications are for energy and decentralization.

Mining in 2009 vs now

When Satoshi Nakamoto mined the first block in January 2009, he used an ordinary laptop CPU. There was no competition, no special hardware, no mining companies. One person, one computer, and a newly born protocol.

Today, Bitcoin mining is a global industry worth billions of dollars. Public mining companies are listed on stock exchanges. Warehouse-sized mining facilities operate in countries with cheap electricity. The hardware used is the ASIC (Application-Specific Integrated Circuit), a chip designed from scratch for one purpose only: to compute SHA-256 hashes as fast as possible. This chip can’t be used for anything else.

Why did this industrialization happen?

The answer is simple: economic incentives. As long as the block reward is valuable enough, there’s strong motivation to invest in more efficient hardware. More efficient miners get a larger share of the reward at a lower cost. This competition drives extraordinarily fast hardware innovation.

But this industrialization also brings consequences worth thinking about. Mining that anyone could once do at home now requires large capital and access to cheap electricity at industrial scale. This creates centralization pressure that conflicts with Bitcoin’s original spirit as a system everyone can participate in.

Bitcoin mining isn’t just "computers solving a puzzle." It’s an industry with a global supply chain, a complex hardware market, and unique economic dynamics. Understanding this industry helps us understand the resilience and vulnerabilities of the Bitcoin network in the real world.

Chapter 11 · 11.2
From laptop to factory — 15 years of the most dramatic hardware evolution in computing history
Evolution of Mining Hardware

The history of Bitcoin mining hardware is one of the most dramatic technology evolution stories in computing history. In less than 15 years, mining efficiency increased trillions of times over.

The CPU era (2009-2010)

In Bitcoin’s early days, everyone mined with an ordinary laptop or desktop CPU. Satoshi Nakamoto himself mined with a CPU. The hash rate of the whole network at the time was so low that anyone could mine a block with a regular computer. There was no meaningful competition and a block could be found in minutes with a standard laptop.

The GPU era (2010-2013)

Someone realized that the GPU (Graphics Processing Unit), the chip usually used for rendering game graphics, was far more efficient at computing SHA-256 than a CPU. A GPU can perform thousands of parallel operations at once. The hash rate jumped drastically and CPU mining was practically no longer profitable.

The FPGA era (2011-2013)

An FPGA (Field-Programmable Gate Array) is a chip that can be reprogrammed for specific tasks. More efficient than a GPU for SHA-256, but still reprogrammable for other purposes. This era was short because it was soon replaced by the ASIC.

The ASIC era (2013-present)

The ASIC (Application-Specific Integrated Circuit) is a chip designed from scratch for one purpose only: to compute SHA-256 hashes as fast and efficiently as possible. It can’t be used for anything else at all. A modern ASIC like the Antminer S21 can reach more than 200 TH/s (terahashes per second) with an energy consumption of around 3,500 watts. This means hundreds of trillions of hash attempts every second.

The ASIC’s impact on decentralization

ASICs create a paradox: on one hand, ASICs make the network far more secure because the very high hash rate makes a 51% attack extremely expensive. On the other hand, ASICs make mining increasingly concentrated among players with large capital to buy hardware and pay for electricity at industrial scale. Mining that anyone could once do at home is now dominated by large mining companies with dedicated facilities.

The evolution of mining hardware reflects a simple economic law: as long as there’s a large enough financial incentive, innovation will keep pushing the limits of efficiency. What’s interesting is how this inadvertently changed who can participate in the Bitcoin mining process.

Chapter 11 · 11.3
Rather than struggling alone, join up and share the rewards
Mining Pools

When mining became very competitive, individual miners faced a problem similar to a gambler’s: they might never find a block even after working hard for months. The probability of finding a block for one small miner is very low, and their income becomes very uncertain. The mining pool emerged as a solution.

What is a mining pool?

A mining pool is a group of miners who combine their hash rate and share the rewards proportionally. Instead of each one struggling alone to find a block, they work together. When the pool finds a block, the reward is split among all members based on each one’s hash rate contribution. The analogy is like a lottery ticket: instead of one person buying one ticket with a very small chance of winning, a hundred people chip in to buy a hundred tickets and share the proceeds. The chance of winning is far higher, and although the prize is split, income becomes far more consistent.

How does a pool work?

The pool gives work to its member miners in the form of tasks that are easier than the actual network target. These tasks are called shares. Miners submit the shares they manage to find to the pool as proof that they really worked. Occasionally, a share a miner finds turns out to also meet the actual network target, meaning a new block is found and the pool gets the reward.

Payment methods

There are several ways pools split rewards. PPS (Pay Per Share): miners are paid for every valid share, regardless of whether the pool finds a block or not, and the pool bears the variance risk. PPLNS (Pay Per Last N Shares): payment is based on the shares contributed in the last period before a block is found, more volatile but advantageous when the pool is lucky. FPPS (Full Pay Per Share): like PPS but also accounts for transaction fees in the split.

The pool’s impact on decentralization

Mining pools create centralization concerns: a few large pools control a significant portion of the network’s total hash rate. But it’s worth noting that individual miners in a pool are still free to switch pools at any time. If a pool acts dishonestly, miners will leave it and move to another pool or mine on their own. Stratum V2 is the newest protocol that gives miners more control, including the ability to choose for themselves which transactions go into a block.

Mining pools are an innovation that makes mining more accessible to small miners, but at the same time create centralization pressure. This is a trade-off that’s continually debated in the Bitcoin community. Stratum V2 is one effort to preserve decentralization while still enabling efficient collaboration.

Chapter 11 · 11.4
Mining is a business — revenue must exceed costs
Mining Economics

Bitcoin mining is a business. Like any other business, its success is determined by one simple equation: revenue must exceed costs. But mining economics is far more complex than an ordinary business because some of its key variables keep changing dynamically.

Sources of miner revenue

Miners get two sources of revenue. First, the block reward: currently 3.125 BTC per block, and it will keep decreasing with every halving. Second, transaction fees: all the fees from transactions included in the block. When the mempool is busy, fees can become a significant portion of total revenue. A miner’s total revenue is calculated in USD or local currency, so the BTC price strongly determines profitability.

Operating costs

The biggest cost in mining is electricity. A modern ASIC needs thousands of watts to operate, and has to run 24 hours a day, 7 days a week. The electricity cost per kilowatt-hour (kWh) is the factor that most distinguishes profitable miners from unprofitable ones. Miners with access to cheap electricity (0.03-0.05 USD/kWh) can stay profitable even when the BTC price is low. Miners with expensive electricity (0.10-0.15 USD/kWh) can lose money even when the BTC price is high. Other costs include the ASIC hardware that needs replacing every few years, cooling infrastructure, facility rent, and labor.

Hash rate and difficulty

As more miners join the network with new hardware, the total hash rate rises and the difficulty adjusts so block time stays at 10 minutes. This means every miner gets a smaller share of the reward. Mining economics is a zero-sum game in the short term: if other miners become more efficient, less efficient miners come under more pressure.

Hashprice and break-even price

Hashprice is a metric that calculates how much USD is generated per terahash per second per day. This lets miners compare profitability regardless of the size of their operation. Break-even price is the minimum BTC price for a mining operation not to lose money, depending on the electricity cost and the efficiency of the hardware used.

Halving and its impact

At every halving, the block reward is cut in half. If the BTC price doesn’t rise enough to offset it, inefficient miners will shut off their hardware. The hash rate can drop temporarily, the difficulty adjusts down, and the remaining miners become more profitable. The halving is one of the most tense moments in Bitcoin’s mining economic cycle.

Mining economics is a very dynamic and self-correcting system. High profitability attracts more miners, which raises the difficulty, which reduces profitability. Low profitability pushes miners out, which lowers the difficulty, which increases profitability for those remaining. This is a balance that keeps moving.

Chapter 11 · 11.5
Large energy consumption — wasteful or meaningful?
Mining and Energy

No topic in the Bitcoin ecosystem is more frequently debated than mining’s energy consumption. Criticism comes from various directions, and the defenses are also varied. What matters is understanding the facts before drawing conclusions.

How much energy does Bitcoin use?

The energy consumption of the Bitcoin network cannot be measured directly, because there is no meter attached to it. All that exists are estimates, and the two most cited ones differ considerably. As of mid-2026 the Cambridge Centre for Alternative Finance estimates around 138 TWh per year, while Digiconomist puts it near 204 TWh. The gap is 66 TWh, and it is not carelessness.

Why two equally credible figures can differ that much

Because they answer slightly different questions. Cambridge builds from the bottom up: surveying mining companies, cataloguing the hardware actually in use, then computing its power draw. Digiconomist builds from the economics: assuming miners spend a fixed share of revenue on electricity, then working backwards. As a result the Digiconomist figure rises when the price rises, while the Cambridge figure stays anchored to physical hardware. Neither is wrong. What is wrong is quoting one number without saying which model it came from.

For context, that figure is roughly the electricity consumption of a mid-sized country such as Poland or Thailand, and sits around half a percent of all electricity worldwide. As another comparison, global data centres consumed roughly 485 TWh in 2025 and are growing about 15 percent a year, while Bitcoin’s figure stays broadly flat.

What will not change, whatever the number becomes

The figures above will certainly date, and any part of this book quoting TWh should be treated as a dated snapshot. But the following three things do not date. First, Bitcoin’s energy use follows miner revenue, not transaction count. Adding or removing transactions within a block does not change the energy at all, because what is paid for is finding the block, not filling it. This is why the widely quoted "energy per transaction" figure misleads: its denominator can move while the numerator does not.

Second, because it follows revenue, it rises and falls with the price. This is not theory: in December 2025 the annualised estimate approached 190 TWh at the market peak, then fell back into the 130s once prices weakened through 2026 and less efficient miners powered down. Third, hardware efficiency keeps improving, from around 98 J/TH in 2016 to roughly 13.5 J/TH in 2026. Energy per hash falls continuously while the number of hashes keeps climbing, and what settles the outcome is the price.

If you want a current figure, what to look for is not news coverage but one of those two indices directly, and what you should record is the date and which model you are quoting.

Energy and security

The energy used in mining isn’t wasted from Bitcoin’s perspective. It’s a cost that directly secures the network. The more energy used for mining, the more expensive it is to attack the network. This is a deliberate security mechanism: the work in proof of work must be real and expensive.

Mining energy sources

The composition of mining energy sources has shifted significantly. Because miners seek the cheapest electricity, they often head toward energy sources that are cheap because they’re in surplus or isolated: surplus hydro energy, flared gas from oil wells, geothermal energy, and other renewable sources. Recent estimates show about 50-60% of mining energy comes from renewable or low-carbon sources, although this figure is debated because of differing methodologies.

Comparison with other systems

Often overlooked in the debate is the energy consumption of the traditional financial system: banks, ATMs, offices, data centers, printing physical money, and transporting cash. This comparison is hard to do apples-to-apples because of the different scope, but it’s important to keep a balanced perspective.

Mining as a flexible energy buyer

There’s an interesting argument that mining can play a positive role in the energy grid. Miners can act as a very flexible energy buyer: they can be turned on when there’s an energy surplus and turned off when demand is high. This helps stabilize the grid and can make renewable energy projects more economical by being an always-present energy buyer even outside peak hours.

The Bitcoin energy debate has no simple answer. How expensive is a decentralized monetary security system worth paying for? This is a question of values, not just technicals. What you can do is make sure this debate is based on accurate facts, not numbers taken without context.

Chapter 11 · 11.6
Between centralization pressure and the spirit of decentralization — where will mining go?
The Future of Mining and Decentralization

Bitcoin mining sits at the intersection of two opposing forces: pressure toward centralization driven by economies of scale, and pressure toward decentralization driven by the spirit of the Bitcoin protocol itself. Where is this industry headed?

Current hash rate concentration

Today, most of the Bitcoin network’s hash rate is concentrated in a few large pools. This doesn’t mean the network is controlled by a handful of parties, because individual miners in a pool are free to switch at any time. But this concentration still creates a theoretical risk and questions about long-term resilience. Geographically, China once dominated with more than 60% of the hash rate, but the mining ban in 2021 drove a massive redistribution. Now the hash rate is spread across the United States, Kazakhstan, Russia, Canada, and many other countries. This broader geographic distribution is generally positive for the network’s resilience.

Who chooses the transactions?

In a traditional pool setup, the pool operator decides which transactions go into a block, not the individual miner running the hardware. For a long time this rarely became a problem because pools generally chose transactions based on the highest fee without filtering content.

But Ocean Pool changed this dynamic. Ocean is a pool that explicitly applies transaction filtering: they reject certain transactions they consider inconsistent with legitimate Bitcoin use, including some types of Ordinals and transactions that use OP_RETURN excessively. This isn’t cheating in a technical sense. By protocol, miners do have the right to choose which transactions go into a block.

But this opens a deeper debate: if large pools start censoring certain transactions based on content rather than just technical validity, is Bitcoin’s censorship-resistance threatened? On one hand, some argue miners have the right to choose based on their preferences. On the other hand, some worry this sets a dangerous precedent: if one pool may censor based on content, what stops other pools from censoring based on other, more political criteria?

Stratum V2 and decentralizing transaction selection

Stratum V2 emerges as a structural answer to this question. As an upgrade to the communication protocol between pool and miner, Stratum V2 gives individual miners the ability to choose for themselves which transactions they want to include in a block. This is a fundamental change: even if the hash rate is concentrated in large pools, the power to choose transactions is redistributed back to individual miners. If Stratum V2 is widely adopted, scenarios like Ocean Pool filtering transactions on behalf of all the miners in the pool become harder to happen without the miners’ explicit consent.

Home mining and small-scale mining

There’s a growing movement to bring mining back to small scale. Devices like the Bitaxe are small open-source ASICs that can run at home with a few watts of power. Their hash rate is very small compared to industrial ASICs, but their contribution to network decentralization is greater than the numbers suggest. The argument: a network with millions of small, geographically dispersed miners is more resistant to attack and regulation than a network that depends on a few hundred large industrial facilities.

Transaction fees as a long-term incentive

The block reward will keep decreasing with every halving. Eventually, there will be no more block reward. Miner incentives will depend entirely on transaction fees. If Bitcoin succeeds in becoming a system used by billions of people, the volume and aggregate fees might be more than enough. But if not, the hash rate could drop and network security could weaken. This is one of the most fundamental questions about Bitcoin’s long-term sustainability, and no one knows the answer for certain.

The future of Bitcoin mining depends on the balance between economic incentives, technological innovation, and community decisions. Questions about who has the right to choose transactions, how decentralized the hash rate should be, and whether fees are enough to replace the block reward are questions that will keep being debated. What’s interesting is that all of this happens without any single party who can decide the answer.
