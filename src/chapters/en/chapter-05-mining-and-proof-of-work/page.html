Chapter 5 · 5.1
How can thousands of parties who don’t know each other agree?
Consensus

Every morning, billions of people around the world wake up and agree on one thing without ever discussing it: what time it is. No one decides this. No meeting, no vote. But everyone in the same time zone sees the same clock and agrees that it’s seven in the morning. This is consensus.

Consensus is everywhere

Consensus is the process by which a group of parties reaches a shared agreement about something. It can happen formally or informally, with a central authority or without. In parliament, consensus is reached through voting. In the stock market, consensus about a stock’s price is reached through continuous buying and selling. On the internet, the TCP/IP protocol is a consensus about how data should be sent between computers. In society, language is a consensus about what a word means.

Easy consensus and hard consensus

Most of the consensus we know has one thing in common: there’s a central authority that serves as the anchor. The central bank sets the value of the currency. The government sets the laws. This authority makes consensus relatively easy. But what if there’s no referee? What if all parties are equal, none has more authority, and some of them might not even be honest? This is hard consensus. And this is exactly the problem Bitcoin faces.

Two consequences of not following consensus

In any consensus-based system, a party that doesn’t follow the shared agreement faces two choices. First, return to consensus. They realize their version differs from the majority, adjust, and continue together. Second, form their own faction. They keep their version, separate from the majority, and form a parallel system. In the Bitcoin world, this is called a fork.

Bitcoin’s consensus problem

Bitcoin has no central authority. What exists is thousands of nodes spread around the world, each keeping its own copy of the blockchain. The question: if two nodes get different information about the same block, who is right? How can thousands of computers that don’t know each other agree on one and the same version of history? Bitcoin’s solution to this problem is Proof of Work, which we’ll cover in 5.2.

Satoshi wasn’t the first to think about the problem of consensus without a central authority. Computer scientists had been wrestling with this since 1982 in what’s called the Byzantine Generals Problem. What Satoshi did was find the first practical solution that could work in an open system, without permission, and without prior trust between parties.

Soft fork dan hard fork

When a change to the rules is proposed in Bitcoin, the way it’s changed determines whether the network stays united or splits permanently.

A soft fork is a rule change that is backward compatible. Old nodes can still communicate with new nodes, but new nodes have stricter rules. The key is this: the new rules are a subset of the old ones, so a block that is valid under the new rules is automatically valid under the old rules too. Old nodes do not reject it. And because the majority of hashrate enforces the new rules, it is their chain that grows longer. Thanks to the longest chain rule, old nodes eventually follow the longer chain, even without having to update their software. SegWit, activated in 2017, is an example of a soft fork.

A hard fork is a rule change that is not backward compatible. Old nodes can’t recognize blocks from new nodes, and vice versa. The split that occurs is permanent. Two chains grow side by side forever, forming two different networks and two different currencies. There’s no longest chain that can reconcile them because they’re already speaking different languages. Bitcoin Cash, which split off from Bitcoin in 2017, is an example of a hard fork.

The key difference: in a soft fork, old nodes don’t need to know there are new rules. In a hard fork, old and new nodes can no longer live on the same network.

Chapter 5 · 5.2
Very hard to create, very easy to verify
Proof of Work

Bitcoin’s consensus requires a way to prove that someone has done real work before they can propose a new block. That way is called Proof of Work.

An asymmetric puzzle

Proof of Work is a mathematical puzzle with a very specific property: very hard to solve, but very easy to verify. Find a number that, when combined with the block data and hashed with SHA-256d, produces a hash starting with a certain number of zeros. The number being sought is called the nonce. There’s no clever way to find it. The only way is to try one by one, billions of times per second, until a hash that meets the requirement is found.

Target and leading zeros

The Bitcoin network sets a target: the hash of the block header must be smaller than a certain number. In practice, this means the hash must start with a certain number of leading zeros. The more zeros required, the harder the puzzle. Currently, a valid Bitcoin block hash must start with around 19 to 20 zeros.

Why is this effective?

Because of the property of hash functions we learned in Chapter 4: the output can’t be predicted. There’s no way to know in advance which nonce will produce a valid hash. Each attempt is independent, the result of the previous attempt doesn’t help at all. This means the only advantage someone can have is computational speed.

A verifiable proof

Once a nonce is found, verifying it takes just a single hash computation. Other nodes simply take the block header, compute SHA-256d, and check whether the result meets the target. One second, done. This is the asymmetry: it takes trillions of attempts to find, but only one attempt to verify.

Proof of Work wasn’t Satoshi’s invention. The concept was first proposed by Cynthia Dwork and Moni Naor in 1993, and popularized by Adam Back in the Hashcash system in 1997 to fight email spam. Satoshi adapted it as a consensus mechanism for Bitcoin, a use that far exceeds its original purpose.

Chapter 5 · 5.3
From transaction to block — the complete process
Mining

We already know that a miner has to find a nonce that makes the block header hash meet the target. But what’s actually inside that block header? And how does the mining process work from start to finish?

The anatomy of a block

Every Bitcoin block consists of two parts: the header and the body. The body contains the list of transactions collected from the mempool. The header contains 6 fields with a total size of exactly 80 bytes, and this is what the miner hashes trillions of times per second.

The mining process from start to finish

First, the miner collects transactions from the mempool and chooses those paying the highest fees. Second, the miner assembles the block candidate: computing the Merkle Root of all chosen transactions and filling in all header fields except the nonce. Third, the miner starts trying nonces from 0, 1, 2, and so on. If the entire nonce space is exhausted without a result, the miner changes the timestamp or the order of transactions, which changes the Merkle Root, then starts again from nonce 0. Fourth, once a valid hash is found, the miner immediately broadcasts the block to the entire network.

Coinbase transaction

Every block always contains one special transaction in the first position: the coinbase transaction. It has no input from any UTXO. It creates new bitcoin from nothing as a reward for the miner who successfully finds the block. The coinbase transaction also contains a free field called the coinbase data. Satoshi filled this field with a famous message in the genesis block: "The Times 03/Jan/2009 Chancellor on brink of second bailout for banks."

80 bytes. That is the size of a Bitcoin block header. No more, no less. The entire security of the network depends on hashing these 80 bytes trillions of times per second, around the world, every ten minutes.

Chapter 5 · 5.4
However many miners come or go, always 10 minutes
Difficulty Adjustment

Bitcoin is designed to produce one block every ten minutes. But how can the network maintain this rhythm when the number of miners keeps changing?

The problem to be solved

If more miners join, the network hashrate rises, and blocks are found faster than ten minutes. If miners leave, the hashrate drops, and blocks are found slower. Without an adjustment mechanism, the interval between blocks would be chaotic.

How difficulty adjustment works

Every 2016 blocks, Bitcoin automatically adjusts the difficulty. The number 2016 isn’t arbitrary: with a target of 10 minutes per block, 2016 blocks should take exactly 2 weeks. The formula is simple: compare the actual time it took to mine the last 2016 blocks with the ideal time. If it was faster, difficulty rises. If it was slower, difficulty falls.

Adjustment limits

To prevent overly extreme changes, Bitcoin limits the adjustment to a maximum of 4× up or 4× down in one period. This protects the network from very sudden surges or drops in hashrate.

Who decides?

No one decides. Each node computes this adjustment independently using the same rules, and the result is always identical. This is another example of consensus without a central authority.

Why ten minutes?

Satoshi chose ten minutes as a compromise. Fast enough that transactions don’t wait too long, but slow enough that a block can spread across the entire network before another miner finds the next block.

Difficulty adjustment is one of the most elegant mechanisms in Bitcoin. It ensures that no matter how much computation is thrown at the network, one block still appears every ten minutes. Bitcoin can’t be spammed with hashrate.

Chapter 5 · 5.5
The only way new bitcoin enters the world
Block Reward and Halving

Every time a miner successfully finds a valid block, they receive two types of income: the block reward and transaction fees. But there’s one part of the block reward that’s unique to Bitcoin: it halves once every four years.

Block reward

The block reward is new bitcoin created from nothing through the coinbase transaction. This is the only way new bitcoin enters circulation. There is no other way. When Satoshi mined the genesis block in January 2009, the block reward was 50 BTC. Every new block added 50 BTC to the circulation.

Halving

Every 210.000 blocks, the block reward is cut in half. With an average of 10 minutes per block, 210.000 blocks take about 4 years. The first halving happened in November 2012: the reward dropped from 50 to 25 BTC. The second halving in July 2016: down to 12,5 BTC. The third halving in May 2020: down to 6,25 BTC. The fourth halving in April 2024: down to 3,125 BTC. This process will continue until around the year 2140.

Why 21 million?

In 1.5 we said there is no definitive explanation for why Satoshi picked 21 million. That still holds, and it does not contradict what follows. What Satoshi never explained is why he picked 50 BTC per block and an interval of 210,000 blocks. Once those two numbers were fixed, 21 million stopped being a choice and became a consequence that follows on its own. If you sum all the eras from 50 BTC, 25 BTC, 12,5 BTC, and so on to infinity, the result approaches exactly 21 million BTC. This is a convergent geometric series.

Formula — Bitcoin total supply

After the reward reaches zero

If the block reward is zero, what motivates miners? Transaction fees. As the reward falls, fees are expected to rise because demand for block space increases. This is a gradual transition over more than 100 years from subsidy to a pure fee economy.

Halving is a disinflation mechanism that’s been programmed from the start. The term is worth getting right: Bitcoin’s supply still grows with every block, only the rate keeps slowing toward zero. What shrinks is the speed of issuance, not the amount in circulation. Unlike a central bank that can print money anytime, Bitcoin’s emission schedule can’t be changed by anyone. Every node verifies that the coinbase transaction doesn’t exceed the allowed reward.

Chapter 5 · 5.6
When an attacker controls the majority of the hashrate
51% Attack

Proof of Work ensures that to change the transaction history, someone would have to repeat all the computational work already done. But there’s one scenario that’s often discussed: what if someone controls more than half of the network’s hashrate?

What can an attacker do?

With more than 50% of the hashrate, an attacker can perform a double-spend. Here’s how: send bitcoin to another party, wait for confirmation, while secretly mining an alternative chain. Because they have more hashrate, the alternative chain grows faster. After receiving the goods, broadcast the alternative chain. Based on the longest chain rule, the network adopts the new chain, and the original payment transaction disappears. The attacker can also censor certain transactions, refusing to include transactions from certain addresses in a block.

What can an attacker not do?

Many people misunderstand this. The attacker can’t steal bitcoin from someone else’s wallet. Can’t forge cryptographic signatures. Can’t print new bitcoin outside the halving schedule. Can’t change the consensus rules. The cryptography underlying Bitcoin is untouched by this attack.

Why does this attack not make economic sense?

To control 51% of Bitcoin’s current hashrate, an attacker would have to operate more ASICs than all the honest miners in the world, with hardware, electricity, and infrastructure costs reaching billions of dollars. If the attack succeeds and is detected, trust in Bitcoin collapses, the price falls, and all the attacker’s hardware loses its value. The attacker destroys their own investment.

What if the motivation isn’t profit, but destruction?

This is a more serious question. Imagine a state-level actor willing to pour almost unlimited resources just to damage Bitcoin, without caring about economic losses. Can Bitcoin survive?

The answer: yes, but it requires an active response from the community. A sustained 51% attack would be detected very quickly. Thousands of nodes around the world would see an unnaturally large reorg. The developer community could propose changing the hashing algorithm through a hard fork. If the algorithm is changed, all the ASIC hardware used by the attacker instantly becomes useless. The attacker would have to buy new hardware all over again, while the community only needs to update software.

This is a deeply asymmetric war: defending is far cheaper than attacking. Every new attack requires a massive new hardware investment. Every response only requires a code update.

The 51% attack and decentralization

This is why decentralized mining isn’t just idealism, but a real defense. The more spread out the hashrate is around the world among thousands of independent operators, the more expensive and harder it is for one party to control the majority. There’s no single point that can be attacked, bribed, or forced to surrender.

Bitcoin’s resilience against a 51% attack isn’t just about cost. It’s also about incentives, decentralization, and the community’s ability to adapt. A network that can change its hashing algorithm within days is a network that’s very hard to destroy permanently, even by an actor with almost unlimited resources.
