Chapter 13 · 13.1
How can a protocol with no owner change?
What is a Soft Fork and a Hard Fork?

We introduced the terms soft fork and hard fork briefly in 5.1 when covering consensus. Here we take both apart properly, because the whole of Chapter 13 rests on the difference. If the opening feels familiar, that is deliberate.

Bitcoin is a living protocol. It needs to evolve to fix bugs, add features, or respond to new threats. But how do you change a protocol that has no owner, no CEO, and is run by thousands of independent nodes around the world? This is where the concept of a fork becomes important.

Hard fork vs soft fork

A hard fork is a protocol change that isn’t backward compatible. Nodes that don’t upgrade can no longer follow the new chain. If some nodes upgrade and some don’t, the network splits into two permanently separate chains. Each has its own longest chain, but they don’t compete with one another because one side’s blocks are irrelevant to the other. A hard fork requires almost universal coordination from the entire ecosystem.

A soft fork is a protocol change that is backward compatible. Old nodes that don’t upgrade can still follow the new chain, because the new rules only tighten the existing rules. A block that’s valid under the new rules is also valid under the old rules. This means it’s still one network with two candidate chains, and the longest one wins. Because the majority of hash rate is usually on the upgraded nodes, their chain is always the longest.

A simple analogy

Imagine a card game with the rule: "a card worth more than 5 wins." A hard fork is changing the rule to "a card worth more than 3 wins"; old players who don’t know the new rule can’t play at the same table. A soft fork is changing the rule to "a card worth more than 5 wins, and it must be red"; the rule becomes stricter, but old players can still play at the same table even if they play suboptimally.

Why Bitcoin prefers soft forks

A soft fork is safer because it doesn’t force all users to upgrade at once. Old nodes can still participate in the network. This reduces the risk of community fracture and allows changes to be made gradually. A hard fork in Bitcoin is considered very risky and is almost never done for new features, only for critical fixes that truly can’t be done with a soft fork.

The choice between a soft fork and a hard fork isn’t just technical, but also political and social. A soft fork allows change without forcing universal consensus, but it also means new features must be designed very carefully to be backward compatible. This is one reason Bitcoin development feels slow compared to other crypto projects.

Chapter 13 · 13.2
No CEO, but there is a process — that’s how ideas get into Bitcoin
BIP — How Ideas Get into Bitcoin

Bitcoin has no CEO, no headquarters, no board of directors. But Bitcoin isn’t without process. There’s a formal mechanism to propose, discuss, and implement changes to the protocol. This mechanism is called a BIP, short for Bitcoin Improvement Proposal.

What is a BIP?

A BIP is a written document that proposes a change or addition to the Bitcoin protocol, its development process, or the standards used. The BIP was first introduced by Amir Taaki in 2011, inspired by the Python Enhancement Proposals (PEP) used by the Python community. Every BIP has a unique number: BIP 32 for HD wallets, BIP 141 for SegWit, BIP 340-342 for Taproot, BIP 352 for Silent Payments. This number becomes the way the community references a proposal consistently.

Who can submit a BIP?

Anyone. A BIP isn’t an exclusive document for core developers. Anyone can write and submit a BIP. But it’s the quality of the argument, the clarity of the technical specification, and support from the community that determine whether a BIP will be considered seriously or ignored.

The BIP process

A submitted BIP first goes through review by a BIP editor who ensures the document’s format and completeness. The editor doesn’t decide whether a BIP is technically good or bad. After being accepted as a draft, the BIP starts being discussed in various forums: the bitcoin-dev mailing list, GitHub, forums, and conferences. This discussion can last months or even years. There’s no formal voting, no single decision. Consensus forms organically from long discussion.

Types of BIPs

There are three types of BIPs. The Standards Track BIP is the most important: it proposes changes to the Bitcoin protocol that affect compatibility, further divided into several layers from the consensus layer to the applications layer. The Informational BIP provides information or guidance without proposing a new feature. The Process BIP proposes changes to Bitcoin’s development process itself.

Why is this process slow?

Because Bitcoin has no central authority that can decide. Every change to a protocol used by millions of people and storing billions of dollars in value must be considered very carefully. One bug in the consensus code could be catastrophic. The slowness is a feature, not a bug.

The BIP is how a decentralized community coordinates change without a central authority. The process is slow and often full of debate, but that’s what makes the legitimacy of the result depend on real consensus, not the decision of one party.

Chapter 13 · 13.3
Who has the power to activate a change?
Types of Soft Fork Activation

A soft fork is clearly safer than a hard fork. But how exactly is a soft fork "activated"? It turns out there are several different activation mechanisms, each with a different trade-off between speed, security, and who has the power to activate a change.

MASF: Miner Activated Soft Fork

MASF is the earliest activation mechanism used. Miners mark the blocks they create with a signal of support for a particular soft fork. This signal is placed in the version field of the block header. If a certain threshold, for example 95% of 2016 blocks in one difficulty period, is reached, the soft fork is activated automatically. The advantage of MASF is that the process is measurable and there’s certainty about when activation happens. Its weakness: miners have a de facto veto.

UASF: User Activated Soft Fork

UASF is a response to MASF’s weakness. Economic nodes, users, exchanges, and businesses that use Bitcoin activate the new rules on a certain date, regardless of whether miners have signaled or not. This creates back-pressure on miners: if miners don’t follow the new rules, their blocks will be rejected by the economic majority of the network, and their reward will be worthless. BIP 148, which activated SegWit in 2017, is the most famous and successful example of UASF.

URSF: User Resisted Soft Fork

URSF is the opposite of UASF. If UASF is users forcing activation, URSF is users forcing rejection. Economic nodes coordinate to reject a soft fork that has been activated by miners but is not wanted by the user community. URSF has never been used fully in Bitcoin’s history, but its existence as an option shows that users have the power to reject a change forced by miners.

Version Bits: How Miners Signal

Modern soft forks use the version bits mechanism defined in BIP 9. The block header has a 32-bit version field. Each bit can be used to signal support for a particular soft fork. This allows several soft forks to run in parallel without conflict. The miner’s signal is indicative, not binding: a miner can signal without actually implementing the new rules.

Speedy Trial: An Evolution of MASF

Speedy Trial is a variation of MASF used to activate Taproot in 2021. Its main idea: give a shorter activation window of about 3 months with a 90% threshold. If the threshold is reached within that window, the soft fork is activated. If not, the process can be repeated with a different mechanism. Speedy Trial worked: Taproot was activated in November 2021 after miners reached the threshold in a relatively short time, without drama like SegWit.

The choice of activation mechanism isn’t just a technical matter. It reflects who is considered most entitled to decide on changes in a decentralized network: the miners who provide computation, or the users who give economic value. Bitcoin’s history shows that both need each other, and no single party can fully impose its will.

Two years of political war that changed how Bitcoin makes decisions
SegWit — The Most Controversial Soft Fork

If there’s one moment in Bitcoin’s history that truly tested whether a decentralized network can make hard decisions without a central authority, it’s the SegWit debate. Running from 2015 to 2017, this wasn’t just a technical debate, it was a political war involving developers, miners, exchanges, users, and Bitcoin businesses around the world.

What is SegWit?

SegWit, short for Segregated Witness, is a soft fork that separates the signature data (witness data) from the main transaction data. Technically, it moves the witness data to a separate part of the block that isn’t counted in the 1 MB block size limit. The result: transactions with SegWit become effectively smaller, increasing network capacity without explicitly changing the 1 MB limit. Besides capacity, SegWit also fixes transaction malleability, a weakness that was one of the main obstacles to implementing the Lightning Network.

Why was it controversial?

The debate began not because of SegWit itself, but because of the larger context: Bitcoin’s scaling debate. Part of the community, especially some miners and large businesses, wanted a simpler solution: just raise the block limit from 1 MB to 2 MB or more through a hard fork. They saw SegWit as too complicated and not enough. On the other side, core developers and most users wanted SegWit because besides capacity, it brought other important improvements and could be done without a risky hard fork.

The New York Agreement and UASF

In May 2017, a large group of Bitcoin businesses and miners signed the New York Agreement, promising to activate SegWit while also raising the block limit to 2 MB through a hard fork, known as SegWit2x. But many Bitcoin Core developers and users rejected this hard fork part. When miners appeared unwilling to activate SegWit without a hard fork guarantee, the user community launched BIP 148: UASF. Nodes running BIP 148 would reject miner blocks that didn’t support SegWit starting August 1, 2017.

The result

The pressure worked. Miners activated SegWit before the August 1 deadline. SegWit officially became active on August 24, 2017 at block 481,824. The hard fork part of the New York Agreement, SegWit2x, was eventually canceled in November 2017 due to insufficient support. Part of the community that wanted large blocks split off to create Bitcoin Cash through a hard fork on August 1, 2017.

SegWit taught several important lessons about Bitcoin governance: miners don’t have an absolute veto, users and economic nodes have real power through UASF, and consensus in Bitcoin can’t be forced by any single party. But the process also showed how hard it is to make a collective decision without a central authority.

From concept to activation in 3 years, without a single drama
Taproot — A Smoother Soft Fork

After the SegWit drama, the Bitcoin community learned a lot about how a major upgrade should not be done. Taproot was a chance to prove that Bitcoin could change in a more mature and coordinated way.

What is Taproot?

Taproot is a package of three BIPs activated together in November 2021: BIP 340 (Schnorr Signatures), BIP 341 (Taproot itself), and BIP 342 (Tapscript). All three are interconnected and designed to work together. Schnorr Signatures replace ECDSA as Bitcoin’s signature scheme. Schnorr is more mathematically efficient and enables signature aggregation: several signatures in one transaction can be combined into a single signature. This reduces transaction size and improves privacy because a multi-signature transaction looks like an ordinary transaction from the outside.

Tapscript introduces a new way to write smart contracts in Bitcoin using the MAST (Merkelized Alternative Script Trees) structure. With MAST, only the script branch that is actually executed needs to be revealed on the blockchain. A complex contract with many conditions looks like an ordinary payment from the outside as long as the executed path is the "happy path".

Why smoother than SegWit?

Taproot was activated using Speedy Trial with a short activation window and a 90% threshold. No UASF drama, no threats, no split. Miners reached the threshold in just 6 weeks after the window opened. Several factors contributed: everyone was already tired of the SegWit drama, there was no controversial trade-off like the block size debate, Taproot’s technical benefits were clear, and Speedy Trial provided a fair mechanism with a clear time limit.

What changed after Taproot?

Taproot became active at block 709,632 on November 14, 2021. Initial adoption was slow because wallets and services needed to be updated to support the new P2TR (Pay-to-Taproot) address format. Taproot opened the way for a more private Lightning Network with PTLCs (Point Time Lock Contracts) replacing HTLCs, and for a richer smart contract ecosystem in Bitcoin.

Taproot is proof that Bitcoin can change without major conflict. But this doesn’t mean Bitcoin governance is already perfect. Taproot succeeded because its benefits weren’t controversial and no major economic interest was threatened. The next, more controversial upgrade could require a process just as hard as SegWit.

Chapter 13 · 13.6
No one decides — everyone influences
Bitcoin Governance — Who Really Decides?

After studying BIPs, soft forks, UASF, SegWit, and Taproot, a reasonable question arises: who actually has the power to change Bitcoin? The answer isn’t simple, and that’s what makes it interesting.

No one decides, everyone influences

Bitcoin has no formal governance. No voting, no board of directors, no defined decision mechanism. But change still happens, through an organic process in which various parties influence each other and ultimately reach consensus or not.

Developers

Developers who contribute to various Bitcoin implementations like Bitcoin Core, Bitcoin Knots, and others are the people who write and review the code that nodes run. They have great influence because they’re the ones who implement technical changes. But they can’t force anyone to run their code, and anyone is free to fork those implementations.

Miners

Miners are the party that produces blocks and secures the network. In MASF, the miners’ signal determines when a soft fork is activated. But the SegWit UASF case proved that miners can be forced by economic nodes: blocks rejected by economic nodes don’t generate real income.

Nodes

Nodes are the most often overlooked but most fundamental actor in Bitcoin governance. There are two kinds: independent nodes run by individuals, communities, or organizations without a direct economic motive, and economic nodes run by exchanges, wallets, and businesses. What matters: one home node has the same weight as one large exchange when it comes to validating and enforcing the consensus rules. They are the true guardians of the rules.

Users and Economic Actors

Ordinary users are the hardest to measure in influence, but in the long term they’re the ones who determine Bitcoin’s value. Economic actors and businesses like exchanges, merchants, payment processors, and institutions have significant economic power: exchanges can determine which chain gets liquidity, merchants who accept Bitcoin give the network real value. But they also have limitations, depending on user trust and unable to force a protocol change directly.

Why can no one force change?

Because Bitcoin is opt-in. Every node is free to choose what software it runs. If an implementation releases a new version the community doesn’t like, people can keep running the old version or fork the implementation. This informal governance process has advantages: there’s no single point of failure and no one can hijack Bitcoin for their own interest. Its drawback: the process is very slow and debates can last years.

Bitcoin governance isn’t a democracy, isn’t an autocracy, and isn’t anarchy. It’s a system where power is spread among many parties that don’t trust each other, but are forced to coordinate because they all depend on the same network. This may be the most interesting governance experiment of the digital era.
